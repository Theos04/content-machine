Type of nuclear fission reactor

[](/wiki/File:Figure_4_Illustration_of_a_light_water_small_modular_nuclear_reactor_\(SMR\)_\(20848048201\).jpg)Illustration of a [light water](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor") small modular nuclear reactor (SMR)

A **small modular reactor** (**SMR**) is an emergent class of [nuclear fission reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_fission_reactor "Nuclear fission reactor") with a [rated electrical power](/wiki/Power_rating "Power rating") of less than 300 [megawatts](/wiki/MWe "MWe") (MWe), which use [modular design](/wiki/Modular_design "Modular design") principles to achieve streamlined construction and enhanced scalability compared to large [light-water reactors](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor").[](#cite_note-Hussein2020-1)[](#cite_note-steigerwald-20231015-2) Many SMR designs are intended to be built in factories and transported to installation sites as [prefabricated](/wiki/Prefabricated "Prefabricated") modules, while others are designed for flexible multi-unit configurations.[](#cite_note-Hussein2020-1)

The term SMR refers to the physical size, electrical capacity, and modular construction approach.[](#cite_note-NuclearNewsSMRHistory-3) Reactor technology varies significantly among SMR designs. As of March 2026, most SMR designs are light-water reactors (LWRs),[](#cite_note-WNASMRs-4) however SMR concepts encompass various reactor types, including [Generation IV](/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor "Generation IV reactor"), [thermal-neutron reactors](/wiki/Thermal-neutron_reactor "Thermal-neutron reactor"), [fast-neutron reactors](/wiki/Fast-neutron_reactor "Fast-neutron reactor"), [molten salt reactor](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor "Molten salt reactor"), and [gas-cooled reactor](/wiki/Gas-cooled_reactor "Gas-cooled reactor") models.[](#cite_note-WNASMRs-4)[](#cite_note-SustainabilityTimes_2019-11-29-5) Many SMRs also incorporate [passive safety](/wiki/Passive_nuclear_safety "Passive nuclear safety") features.[](#cite_note-Hussein2020-1)

Commercial SMRs are designed to deliver an [electrical power](/wiki/Electrical_power "Electrical power") output as low as 10 MWe and up to 300 MWe per module.[[a]](#cite_note-7) Reactors below 10 MWe in capacity are considered [nuclear microreactors](/wiki/Nuclear_microreactor "Nuclear microreactor").[](#cite_note-8) SMRs may also be designed purely for [desalination](/wiki/Desalination "Desalination") or [process heat](/wiki/Process_heat "Process heat") rather than electricity. These SMRs are measured in [megawatts thermal](/wiki/Watt#MWt "Watt") (MWt). Many SMR designs plan for customers to simply add modules to achieve a desired electrical output rather than scaling the size of the reactor.[](#cite_note-SMRs3-9): 53 These reactors are also expected to enhance safety through [passive safety](/wiki/Passive_nuclear_safety "Passive nuclear safety") systems that operate without external power or human intervention during emergency scenarios, although this is not specific to SMRs but rather a characteristic of most modern reactor designs. SMRs are also claimed to have lower power plant staffing costs, as their operation is fairly simple,[](#cite_note-auto22-10)[](#cite_note-:0b-11) and are claimed to have the ability to bypass financial and safety barriers that inhibit the construction of conventional reactors.[](#cite_note-:0b-11)[](#cite_note-:1-12)

SMRs have attracted strong interest from technology companies, such as [Google](/wiki/Google "Google") and [Microsoft](/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft"), for use in powering [data centers](/wiki/Data_center "Data center") to meet demand driven by the [AI boom](/wiki/AI_boom "AI boom").[](#cite_note-NYTDataCenters-13) Modular reactors are expected to reduce construction costs and time compared to large light-water reactors, as well as allow data center operators to draw power exclusively from a behind-the-meter SMR without purchasing electricity from the [power grid](/wiki/Power_grid "Power grid").[](#cite_note-POWERDataCenters-14)

## Definition

According to the [American Nuclear Society](/wiki/American_Nuclear_Society "American Nuclear Society") (ANS), while small or modular reactor concepts date back to the 1950s, the term "small modular reactor" first entered common use in the late 1970s.[](#cite_note-ANSTBT-15) Since then, the definition of an SMR has remained somewhat controversial.[](#cite_note-Trikouros2012-16)

John Fabian, writing in _Nuclear Newswire_ , stated in 2026 that until around 2011, the abbreviation "SMR" referred either to "small modular reactor" or "small and medium sized reactor". According to Fabian, "the way 'SMR' is being used today does not seem consistent and is design dependent."[](#cite_note-NuclearNewsSMRHistory-3) For example, as of 2026 the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) defines SMRs as only light-water reactors under 300 MWe, while non-LWR designs are considered "advanced reactors".[](#cite_note-NuclearNewsSMRHistory-3)[](#cite_note-NRCAdvancedReactors-17) At the same time, the World Nuclear Association defines SMRs as any reactor under 300 MWe so long as it is designed with modular technology.[](#cite_note-WNASMRs-4) According to Fabian, the classification of reactors as SMRs based on their power output has remained consistent, with reactors under 300 MWe being classified as SMRs.[](#cite_note-NuclearNewsSMRHistory-3)

As of 2023, there was broad consensus in the nuclear industry that SMRs are defined as nuclear reactors designed intentionally for low power (below 300 MWe) and with modular design applied either to individual components or the entire assembly.[](#cite_note-steigerwald-20231015-2)

## Operational SMRs

As of 2026[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Small_modular_reactor&action=edit), only China and Russia have successfully built operational SMRs.[](#cite_note-CNBC-18)[](#cite_note-19) Russia has been operating a floating nuclear power plant [Akademik Lomonosov](/wiki/Akademik_Lomonosov "Akademik Lomonosov"), in Russia's Far East ([Pevek](/wiki/Pevek "Pevek")), commercially since 2020.[](#cite_note-klt-40_pris-20) China's [pebble-bed](/wiki/Pebble-bed_reactor "Pebble-bed reactor") modular high-temperature [gas-cooled reactor](/wiki/Gas-cooled_reactor "Gas-cooled reactor") [HTR-PM](/wiki/HTR-PM "HTR-PM") was connected to the grid in 2021.[](#cite_note-nei-20230118-21)

As of 2025, there were 127 [modular reactor designs](/wiki/List_of_small_modular_reactor_designs "List of small modular reactor designs"), with seven designs operating or under construction, 51 in the pre-licensing or licensing process, and 85 designers in discussions with potential site owners.[](#cite_note-wnn-20250723-22)

## Background

While small modular reactors have experienced a resurgence in the 2000s, they are not a novel concept, and have been under development for many decades.[](#cite_note-NuclearPowerReactorDesigns-23)

### Early small reactors

Small reactors have been used for military use since the 1950s for [nuclear marine propulsion](/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion "Nuclear marine propulsion").[](#cite_note-WNA-NMP-24) The thermal output of the largest naval reactor as of 2025 is estimated at 700 MWt (the [A1B reactor](/wiki/A1B_reactor "A1B reactor")), similar in capacity to some large SMR plant designs. Naval nuclear reactors have had an excellent record of safety. According to public information, the US Navy's [Naval Reactors](/wiki/Naval_Reactors "Naval Reactors") program has never succumbed to a meltdown or radioactive release over its 60 years of service. In 2003, Admiral [Frank Bowman](/wiki/Frank_Bowman "Frank Bowman") backed up the Navy's claim by testifying no such accident has ever occurred.[](#cite_note-25) Several nuclear-powered commercial vessels have also been constructed, including four nuclear freighters and nine nuclear icebreakers.[](#cite_note-:24-6)

[Lawrence R. Hafstad](/wiki/Lawrence_R._Hafstad "Lawrence R. Hafstad"), the Director of Reactor Development at the [United States Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission"), proposed building small, cheap, transportable nuclear power packages for use in remote areas.[](#cite_note-Weinberg1952-26)[](#cite_note-Murray1954-27) [Alvin M. Weinberg](/wiki/Alvin_M._Weinberg "Alvin M. Weinberg"), Director of Research at [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Laboratory "Oak Ridge National Laboratory") (ORNL), wrote in support of Hafstad's proposal in 1952, adding that the naval reactors could provide a basis for developing nuclear power packages. He suggested that building many small reactors for remote sites would reduce the financial risk of establishing a nuclear industry:[](#cite_note-Weinberg1952-26)

> The main advantage of the power-package approach to establishment of a nuclear energy industry is that the technology would rely on comparatively many small units, rather than on a very few enormous ones. Thus industry would not try to hit the jackpot right off, but would edge into the business a little at a time and at each stage would be able to match its risk with its financial capability. 

— Alvin Weinberg, "Wanted: Smaller and More Reactors", _Nucleonics_ (November 1952)

Between 1954 and 1977, the [US Army](/wiki/US_Army "US Army") experimented with powering military installations with small land-based reactors during the [Army Nuclear Power Program](/wiki/Army_Nuclear_Power_Program "Army Nuclear Power Program").[](#cite_note-WNASMRs-4) The preliminary design for one such reactor was produced by ORNL under Weinberg's supervision.[](#cite_note-28) One of the Army reactors, PM-2A, was noted as extensively utilizing prefabricated modules to achieve lower construction cost.[](#cite_note-29)

Military small reactors are quite different from commercial SMRs. Historically, the military relied on [highly enriched uranium](/wiki/Highly_enriched_uranium "Highly enriched uranium") (HEU) to power their reactors and not the [low-enriched uranium](/wiki/Low-enriched_uranium "Low-enriched uranium") (LEU) fuel used by SMRs. This is because submarine reactors are severely space-constrained and require higher power density than civilian reactors.[](#cite_note-WNA-NMP-24) Many naval reactors also operate for over a decade or more without refueling.[](#cite_note-WNA-NMP-24)

Early land-based reactors constructed in the United States were relatively small, such as the 60 MW [Shippingport Atomic Power Station](/wiki/Shippingport_Atomic_Power_Station "Shippingport Atomic Power Station") and the 250 MW [Indian Point Unit 1](/wiki/Indian_Point_Energy_Center "Indian Point Energy Center"). However, these reactors were intended to be scaled up into large central-station power plants, to take advantage of economies of scale. Soon after the first successful nuclear demonstrations, utilities raced to scale up plants before operational experience could be gained from smaller units.[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30): 28–29 While traditional engineering wisdom dictates that a system should be scaled up twofold, by the time many nuclear plants with capacities exceeding 1000 MW were being ordered and constructed, no reactor larger than 250 MW was operational.[[b]](#cite_note-31) While nuclear power plants were formerly designed as effectively standardized designs, the growth of operational issues and new safety requirements led to plants being designed and built as "one-of-a-kind" designs, further increasing cost, construction, and licensing issues.[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30) Reactor orders soon fell off sharply, and none of the plants ordered after 1974, except [Watts Bar Unit 2](/wiki/Watts_Bar_Nuclear_Plant "Watts Bar Nuclear Plant"), were ever constructed. The nuclear industry, throughout the following decades, would focus on optimizing the existing large reactors, whose average capacity factors rose from 50% to 90% by the mid-2000s.[](#cite_note-SMRs3-9)

### Renewed interest

In 1982, the [Electric Power Research Institute](/wiki/Electric_Power_Research_Institute "Electric Power Research Institute") (EPRI) conducted a study that recommended smaller, less capital-intensive, and inherently safe reactors be designed instead of large light-water reactors. Weinberg, then the director of the Institute for Energy Analysis, conducted a related study into inherently safe reactors. It concluded that such a system was possible, and selected the 400 MW Swedish Process Inherent Ultimate Safety (PIUS) and 100 MW American Modular High Temperature Gas-cooled Reactor (MHTGR) designs as the most intrinsically safe.[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30) At this time, multiple high-temperature gas-cooled reactors were being designed with the intention of building a large plant out of several modular low-power reactors.[](#cite_note-32) The PIUS reactor,[](#cite_note-33) with its reactor vessel situated within a large pool of borated water, and the robust TRISO fuel of the [General Atomics](/wiki/General_Atomics "General Atomics") MHTGR influenced subsequent SMR design features. Several small prefabricated reactor designs were developed around the same time in the UK, France, and Germany, however none were constructed. The EPRI study also led to the [Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy")'s (DOE) Advanced Light Water Reactor program, which resulted in the development of two 600 MW LWRs, the General Electric [ABWR](/wiki/ABWR "ABWR") and later SBWR, and the Westinghouse [AP-600](/wiki/AP600 "AP600") designs. However, these were later scaled up significantly into the [ESBWR](/wiki/ESBWR "ESBWR") and [AP-1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") reactors.[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30) Two AP-1000 reactors were later constructed as [Vogtle Units 3 and 4](/wiki/Vogtle_Electric_Generating_Plant#Units_3_and_4 "Vogtle Electric Generating Plant"), together costing $30 billion due to delays and cost overruns. 

The DOE also ran an Advanced Liquid-Metal Reactor program, which led to the development of the Power Reactor Inherently Safe Module ([PRISM](/wiki/PRISM_\(reactor\) "PRISM \(reactor\)")) SMR by General Electric in the 1980s and 1990s.[](#cite_note-34)[](#cite_note-35) Each 1400 MW PRISM plant would be composed of nine 160 MW reactor modules, which would each be factory-fabricated and shipped by rail to the construction site.[](#cite_note-36) PRISM's use of multiple factory-fabricated modules to comprise a large power plant and its extensive use of passive safety features were both carried into later SMR designs.[](#cite_note-Ingersoll_2009_589–603-37)[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30) In 1983, the International Atomic Energy Agency started the Small and Medium Power Reactor Project Initiation Study to survey designs for small and medium sized reactors. The [Nuclear Energy Agency](/wiki/Nuclear_Energy_Agency "Nuclear Energy Agency") launched a follow-on study in 1991 that evaluated 17 small or medium-sized reactor concepts, including several from the DOE's Advanced Light Water and Liquid-Metal programs.[](#cite_note-SMRs2-30)

Government support for reactor design steadily declined, leading to the DOE being issued no budget for nuclear development at all in 1998. However, in 1999 the DOE began the Nuclear Energy Research Initiative (NERI), followed by the [Generation IV International Forum](/wiki/Generation_IV_International_Forum "Generation IV International Forum") in 2000, and later the Global Nuclear Energy Partnership (GNEP) in 2006. The NERI program resulted in the development of multiple SMR designs, including liquid metal-cooled reactors and SMRs designed for process heat, as well as others based on existing LWR technology.[](#cite_note-SMRs3-9) In 2001, a report to Congress examining 50 MWe small modular reactors for powering remote areas, "found no substantive technical issues to hinder development and deployment of SMRs, and initial estimates of the electricity generation costs are comparable to, if not better than, those for current electricity supplies in typical remote areas."[](#cite_note-Report_to_Congress-38) One concept, the IRIS SMR, would later receive significant commercial interest and funding under the GNEP, while another, the MASLWR, later formed the basis for NuScale's SMR. The GNEP and its associated programs also catalyzed significantly greater industry and utility interest in developing SMRs.[](#cite_note-SMRs3-9)

The term "small modular reactors" as opposed to "small-and-medium-sized reactors" was brought to wider use when US Secretary of Energy [Steven Chu](/wiki/Steven_Chu "Steven Chu") identified "small modular reactors" as "America's new nuclear option" in a 2010 _[Wall Street Journal](/wiki/Wall_Street_Journal "Wall Street Journal")_ op-ed, where he stated "SMRs would be ready to 'plug and play' upon arrival [on site]" and be more affordable.[](#cite_note-steigerwald-20231015-2) He announced that [President Barack Obama](/wiki/President_Barack_Obama "President Barack Obama") had requested $39 million for a new SMR design and licensing program.[](#cite_note-wsj-20100323-39)

However, the reactors that were ordered at the time as part of the [expected nuclear renaissance](/wiki/Nuclear_renaissance_in_the_United_States "Nuclear renaissance in the United States") were all large light-water plants. Almost all of these projects failed, largely due to slow federal funding, little growth in electricity consumption, and the [2008 financial crisis](/wiki/2008_financial_crisis "2008 financial crisis"), the effects of which were exacerbated by the high cost and financial risk of the plants.[](#cite_note-NuclearPowerReactorDesigns-23) The Fukushima accident in 2011, while resulting in a significant loss of interest in nuclear energy, drew increased attention to SMRs. In 2012, the DOE began its SMR program in earnest, by which point interest in SMRs was significant.[](#cite_note-SMRs3-9)

### Hope of enhanced safety and reduced costs

Economic factors of scale mean that nuclear reactors tend to be large, to such an extent that size itself becomes a limiting factor.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Furthermore, the 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster "Chernobyl disaster") caused a major setback for the nuclear industry, with worldwide suspension of development, cuts in funding, and closure of reactor plants.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Proponents claim that SMRs would be less expensive due to the application of standardized modules that could be industrially produced off-site in a dedicated factory.[](#cite_note-small-beautiful-40) SMRs do, however, also have economic disadvantages.[](#cite_note-base_report-41) Several studies suggest that the overall costs of SMRs are comparable with those of conventional large reactors. Moreover, extremely limited information about SMR modules transportation has been published.[](#cite_note-transportation2-42) Critics say that modular building will only be cost-effective for a high number of the same SMR type, given the still remaining high costs for each SMR.[](#cite_note-43) A high market share is thus needed to obtain sufficient orders. 

### Contribution to the net zero emissions pathways

In February 2024, the [European Commission](/wiki/European_Commission "European Commission") recognized SMR technology as an important contributor to decarbonization as part of the [EU Green Deal](/wiki/EU_Green_Deal "EU Green Deal").[](#cite_note-44)

In its pathway to reach global [net zero emissions](/wiki/Net_zero_emissions "Net zero emissions") by 2050, the [International Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency") (IEA) considers that worldwide nuclear power should be doubled between 2020 and 2050.[](#cite_note-IEA2022-45) Antonio Vaya Soler, an expert from the [Nuclear Energy Agency](/wiki/Nuclear_Energy_Agency "Nuclear Energy Agency") (NEA), agrees that although renewable energy is essential to fight [global warming](/wiki/Global_warming "Global warming"), it will not be sufficient to achieve net zero [CO2](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") emissions and nuclear energy capacity should be at least doubled.[](#cite_note-VayaSoler2024-46)

To produce the same electrical power as the ~ 400 large nuclear power reactors in the world today, BASE, the German Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management, warns that it would be necessary to build several thousand to tens of thousands of SMRs.[](#cite_note-BASE-47)[](#cite_note-BASE_report2021-48)

Several fleets of SMRs of exactly the same type, industrially manufactured in large numbers, should be rapidly deployed worldwide to significantly reduce emissions of CO2. The Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA) launched at [COP 28](/wiki/COP_28 "COP 28") an initiative _Accelerating SMRs for Net Zero_ to foster collaboration between research organizations, nuclear industry, safety authorities, and governments, in order to reduce [carbon emissions](/wiki/Carbon_emissions "Carbon emissions") to net zero before 2050 to limit [global surface temperature](/wiki/Global_surface_temperature "Global surface temperature") increase.[](#cite_note-NEA2023a-49)[](#cite_note-NEA2023b-50)[](#cite_note-NEA2023c-51)

### Future challenges

Proponents say that nuclear energy with proven technology can be safer; the nuclear industry contends that smaller size will make SMRs even safer than larger conventional plants. This is because the main problem associated with nuclear meltdowns is the [decay heat](/wiki/Decay_heat "Decay heat") that is present after reactor shutdown, which would be much lower for SMRs because of their lower power output. Critics say that many more[](#cite_note-BASE-47) small nuclear reactors pose a higher risk, requiring more transportation of [nuclear fuel](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel "Nuclear fuel") and also increasing the production of [radioactive waste](/wiki/Radioactive_waste "Radioactive waste").[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) SMRs require new designs with new technology, the safety of which has yet to be proven. 

SMRs remain facing a distinct engineering risk of corrosion affecting critical systems and materials. Particularly in SMR systems that use liquid metals or molten salts cooling techniques.[](#cite_note-SMRCorrosion-53) Lack of a licensing process and safety framework has left limited SMRs in preventing potential corrosion levels produced in alternative SMR designs.[](#cite_note-SMRCorrosion-53)

Until 2020, no truly modular SMRs had been commissioned for commercial use.[](#cite_note-Mignacca2-54) In May 2020, the first prototype of a [floating nuclear power plant](/wiki/Floating_nuclear_power_plant "Floating nuclear power plant") with two 30 MWe reactors – the type _[KLT-40](/wiki/KLT-40 "KLT-40")_ – started operation in [Pevek](/wiki/Pevek "Pevek"), Russia.[](#cite_note-klt-40_pris-20) This concept is based on the design of nuclear [icebreakers](/wiki/Icebreaker "Icebreaker").[](#cite_note-klt40-55) The operation of the first commercial land-based, 125 MWe demonstration reactor _[ACP100](/wiki/ACP100 "ACP100")_ (Linglong One) is due to start in China by the end of 2026.[](#cite_note-containment-linglong1-56)

The introduction of SMRs has sparked social and institutional concern. Nuclear projects are of policy agendas, meaning centralization of SMRs. The distribution of SMRs has culminated in criticism and discussion of risk towards communities affected by lack of flexible energy.[](#cite_note-:3-57) As any other energy source, communities are left out and potential environmental issues are needed to be assessed given the rate of expansion of SMR.[](#cite_note-:3-57) In 2026, [Natixis Corporate and Investment Banking](/wiki/BPCE_Group "BPCE Group") reported that the SMR sector was still far from commercial reality and was entering a phase where engineering ambition must confront regulatory complexity, financing realities and industrial execution.[](#cite_note-home-20260602-58)

## Designs

[](/wiki/File:Fission_chain_reaction.svg)A [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") chain is required to generate [nuclear power](/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power").

SMRs are envisioned in multiple designs. Some are simplified versions of current reactors, others involve entirely new technologies.[](#cite_note-59) All proposed SMRs use [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") with designs including [thermal-neutron reactors](/wiki/Thermal-neutron_reactor "Thermal-neutron reactor") and [fast-neutron reactors](/wiki/Fast-neutron_reactor "Fast-neutron reactor"). 

### Thermal-neutron reactors

Thermal-neutron reactors rely on a [moderator](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") (water, [graphite](/wiki/Graphite "Graphite"), [beryllium](/wiki/Beryllium "Beryllium")...) to slow [neutrons](/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") and generally use [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium") as [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material"). Most conventional operating reactors are of this type. 

### Fast reactors

[Fast reactors](/wiki/Fast-neutron_reactor "Fast-neutron reactor") do not use moderators. Instead, they rely on highly enriched uranium (HEU) fuel to absorb [fast neutrons](/wiki/Fast_neutron "Fast neutron"). This usually means changing the fuel arrangement within the core, or using different fuels. E.g., [239  
Pu](/wiki/Plutonium "Plutonium") is more likely to absorb a fast neutron than 235  
U. 

Fast reactors can also be [breeder reactors](/wiki/Breeder_reactor "Breeder reactor"). These reactors release enough neutrons to [transmute](/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation "Nuclear transmutation") [non-fissionable elements](/wiki/Fertile_material "Fertile material") into fissionable ones. A common use for a breeder reactor is to surround the core by a "blanket" of 238  
U, the most easily available isotope. Once the 238  
U undergoes a [neutron absorption](/wiki/Neutron_absorption "Neutron absorption") reaction, it becomes 239  
Pu, which can be removed from the reactor during refueling, and subsequently [reprocessed](/wiki/Nuclear_reprocessing "Nuclear reprocessing") and used as fuel.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear1-60)

## Technologies

[](/wiki/File:Pumpless_light_water_reactor.jpg)Diagram of NRC approved SMR type: Pumpless [light water reactor](/wiki/Light_water_reactor "Light water reactor") developed by [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power") as mini nuclear reactor.

### Coolant

Conventional [light-water reactors](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor") typically use water as a coolant and neutron moderator.[](#cite_note-nrc-201411052-61) SMRs may use water, [liquid metal](/wiki/Liquid_metal_cooled_reactor "Liquid metal cooled reactor"), [gas](/wiki/Gas_cooled_reactor "Gas cooled reactor") and [molten salt](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor "Molten salt reactor") as coolants.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear22-62) Coolant type is determined based on the reactor type, reactor design, and the chosen application. Large-rated reactors primarily use light water as coolant, allowing for this cooling method to be easily applied to SMRs. [Helium](/wiki/Helium "Helium") is often elected as a gas coolant for SMRs because it yields a high plant [thermal efficiency](/wiki/Thermal_efficiency "Thermal efficiency") and supplies a sufficient amount of reactor heat. [Sodium](/wiki/Sodium "Sodium"), [lead](/wiki/Lead "Lead"), and [lead-bismuth eutectic](/wiki/Lead-bismuth_eutectic "Lead-bismuth eutectic") (LBE) are liquid metal coolants studied for 4th generation SMRs. There was a large focus on sodium during early work on large-rated reactors which has since carried over to SMRs to be a prominent choice as a liquid metal coolant.[](#cite_note-:24-6) SMRs have lower cooling water requirements, which expands the number of sites where a SMR could be built, including remote areas typically incorporating [mining](/wiki/Mining "Mining") and [desalination](/wiki/Desalination "Desalination").[](#cite_note-63)

### Thermal/electrical generation

Some gas-cooled reactor designs could drive a [gas turbine](/wiki/Gas_turbine "Gas turbine"), rather than boiling water, such that thermal energy can be used directly. Heat could also be used in [hydrogen production](/wiki/Hydrogen_production "Hydrogen production") and other industrial operations,[](#cite_note-world-nuclear22-62) such as desalination and the production of [petroleum](/wiki/Petroleum "Petroleum") derivative (extracting oil from [oil sands](/wiki/Oil_sands "Oil sands"), making [synthetic oil from coal](/wiki/Coal_liquefaction "Coal liquefaction"), etc.).[](#cite_note-64)

### Load following

SMR designs are generally expected to provide [base load](/wiki/Base_load "Base load") electrical power; some proposed designs are aimed to adjust their power output based on electricity demand.[](#cite_note-65)

Another approach, especially for SMRs designed to provide high temperature heat, is to adopt [cogeneration](/wiki/Cogeneration "Cogeneration"), maintaining consistent heat output, while diverting otherwise unneeded heat to an auxiliary use. [District heating](/wiki/District_heating "District heating"), desalination and hydrogen production have been proposed as cogeneration options.[](#cite_note-Cogeneration2-66)

Overnight desalination requires sufficient freshwater storage capacity to deliver water at times other than when it is produced.[](#cite_note-LoadFollowing2014-67) [Reverse osmosis](/wiki/Reverse_osmosis "Reverse osmosis") membrane and thermal [evaporators](/wiki/Evaporator_\(marine\) "Evaporator \(marine\)") are the two main techniques for [seawater](/wiki/Seawater "Seawater") desalination. The membrane desalination process uses only electricity to power water pumps and is the most employed of the two methods. In the thermal process, the feed water stream is evaporated in different stages with continuous decreases in pressure between the stages. The thermal process directly uses thermal energy and avoids the conversion of thermal power into electricity. Thermal desalination is further divided into two main technologies: the [multi-stage flash distillation](/wiki/Multi-stage_flash_distillation "Multi-stage flash distillation") (MSF) and the Multi-Effect Desalination (MED).[](#cite_note-LoadFollowing2014-67)

## Nuclear safety

A report by the German Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management (BASE) considering 136 different historical and current reactors and SMR concepts stated: "Overall, SMRs could potentially achieve [safety](/wiki/Nuclear_safety_and_security "Nuclear safety and security") advantages compared to power plants with a larger power output, as they have a lower radioactive inventory per reactor and aim for a higher safety level especially through simplifications and an increased use of passive systems. In contrast, however, various SMR concepts also favour reduced regulatory requirements, for example, with regard to the required degree of redundancy or diversity in safety systems. Some developers even demand that current requirements be waived, for example in the area of internal accident management or with reduced planning zones, or even a complete waiver of external emergency protection planning. Since the safety of a reactor plant depends on all of these factors, based on the current state of knowledge it is not possible to state, that a higher safety level is achieved by SMR concepts in principle."[](#cite_note-base_sicherheit-68)[](#cite_note-fortschritt_sz-2021-69)[](#cite_note-base_report-41)

Negative [temperature coefficients](/wiki/Temperature_coefficient#Temperature_coefficient_of_reactivity "Temperature coefficient") in the moderators and the fuels keep the fission reactions under control, causing the reaction to slow as temperature increases.[](#cite_note-70) After the shutdown of a nuclear reactor, the reactor needs to be cooled continuously in order to dissipate decay heat. A loss of emergency cooling such as in the [Fukushima nuclear accident](/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_accident "Fukushima nuclear accident") and the [Three Mile Island accident](/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident "Three Mile Island accident") can result in a [nuclear meltdown](/wiki/Nuclear_meltdown "Nuclear meltdown") when the temperature in the reactor becomes too high. Since the initial decay heat is a fraction of the reactor operating power, the lower operating power of SMRs makes them much safer since less heat needs to be dissipated.[](#cite_note-71)

Some SMR designs proposes cooling systems only based on thermoconvection – natural circulation – to eliminate cooling pumps that could break down. Convection can keep removing decay heat after reactor shutdown. However, some SMRs may need an active cooling system to back up the passive system, increasing cost.[](#cite_note-UCS1-72)

Some SMR designs feature an integral design of which the primary reactor core, steam generator and the pressurizer are integrated within the sealed reactor vessel. This integrated design allows for the reduction of a possible accident as contamination leaks could be contained. In comparison to larger reactors having numerous components outside the reactor vessel, this feature increases the safety by decreasing the risks of an uncontained accident. Some SMR designs also envisage to install the reactor and the spent-fuel storage pools underground.[](#cite_note-:14-73)

Several molten salt reactors are being developed as SMRs, but they are not a new concept. Operational as research and test plants since the 1950s, molten salt reactors are now being touted as a clean and safe alternative to traditional water-cooled SMRs. One of the earliest molten salt reactor experiments was operated at Tennessee's Oak Ridge for four years, but shut down in 1969 after going critical. Even though the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment did end in a critical event, it was well known and respected throughout the nuclear research community as a success. However, later studies found the reactor only operated around 40 percent of the time, and experienced 171 unplanned shutdowns. These shutdowns were attributed to a number of technical problems, including: chronic pipe plugging, which led to charcoal beds designed to capture and remove radioactive materials; blower failures designed to remove reactor heat; and leaks within the freeze-valve safety system allowing fuel escapes. So far, modern metals have proven incapable of sustaining the natural corrosiveness of a small reactor's molten salt over a 4 year application.[](#cite_note-74) Concerning avoiding the risk of corrosion especially occurs and causes serious problem at thin-thickness wall of pipes in heat exchanger, new design named UNOMI has proposed which eliminates primary circuit existing outside of reactor vessel.[](#cite_note-75) In this design, generated heat is removed from the surface of the reactor vessel instead of heat exchanger. As a result, available heat production will be limited to abote a few 10 MWth suitable for SMR. 

Even Fluoride-Salt-Cooled High-Temperature Reactors (FHR) suffer from internal buildups of fission products, clogging cooling and safety systems. A method of reductive extraction can be used to catch buildups before they occur. This method removes the uranium fuel before the fission products. Unfortunately, the gas produced from the fluoride is highly corrosive and exposes plant metal to damage. As an alternative, nitrogen trifluoride is being proposed. However, ongoing research has not proven this to be a viable alternative and its efficacy is unclear.[](#cite_note-76)

## Radioactive waste

New technology in nuclear waste recycling is promising safer and less expensive alternatives to today's methods. Known as partitioning and transmutation (P&T), this recycling and waste reducing process can reduce spent fuel to a smaller volume of waste with considerably less radiotoxicity.[](#cite_note-77)

A chemical separation process is used in P&T to extract plutonium and minor actinides. A specially designed reactor is then used to perform the transmutation of transuranic elements (neptunium, plutonium, americium and curium). Fission is finally applied to safely destroy the remaining elements. P&T is believed to improve radioactive waste management due to the expected reduction in overall waste volume P&T creates. 

Even highly enriched uranium reactors, applying shorter fuel cycle technologies, are now recycling major and minor actinides without the need for high purification schemes. The method is now used by LWR fast reactors in France, India, Japan and the Russian Federation. Their waste requires no plutonium separation from the other actinides. Pyroprocessing spent fuel is currently under development for LWR fast reactors and now operational in India, the Russian Federation and the European Union. Because SMR technology is so new, P&T has yet to be used on the spent fuel these plants will create. However, it is likely to be an important recycling method for most SMRs as this technology develops.[](#cite_note-78)

The back end of the nuclear fuel cycle for SMRs is a complex and contested issue that remains under debate.[](#cite_note-IAEA2023-79)[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) The quantity and radiotoxicity of the radioactive waste produced by SMRs depend primarily on their design and the corresponding fuel cycle. Because SMRs encompass a broad spectrum of nuclear reactor types, there is no simple answer to this issue. SMRs may include small [light water reactors](/wiki/Light_water_reactor "Light water reactor") of the third generation, as well as small fast neutron reactors of the fourth generation. 

Some startup companies developing unconventional SMR prototypes often advocate waste reduction as a key advantage of their proposed solutions, and in some cases claim that their technology could eliminate the need for a deep [geological repository](/wiki/Geological_repository "Geological repository") to dispose of high-level and long-lived radioactive waste.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear1-60)[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) This is particularly true for companies developing fast neutron reactors of the fourth generation, such as molten salt reactors and metal-cooled reactors, including the [sodium-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Sodium-cooled_fast_reactor "Sodium-cooled fast reactor") and [lead-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor "Lead-cooled fast reactor").[](#cite_note-OECD2021-80)[](#cite_note-IAEA2020-81)

Fast breeder reactors "burn" [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") (0.7% of [natural uranium](/wiki/Natural_uranium "Natural uranium")) as fuel, but they also convert [fertile materials](/wiki/Fertile_material "Fertile material") such as [238  
U](/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238") (which makes up 99.3% of natural uranium) into [fissile](/wiki/Fissile "Fissile") [239  
Pu](/wiki/Plutonium-239 "Plutonium-239"). This newly produced plutonium can then be used as nuclear fuel.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear1-60) The [traveling wave reactor](/wiki/Traveling_wave_reactor "Traveling wave reactor") proposed by [TerraPower](/wiki/TerraPower "TerraPower") is designed to "burn" the fuel it breeds in situ, without requiring its removal from the reactor core or further reprocessing.[](#cite_note-82)

Some SMR designs are based on the [thorium fuel cycle](/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle "Thorium fuel cycle"), which is advocated by their promoters as a way to reduce the long-term radiotoxicity of waste compared to the uranium cycle.[](#cite_note-WASH1097-83) However, the thorium cycle also presents significant operational challenges due to the production and use of [232  
U](/wiki/Uranium-232 "Uranium-232") and the long-lived fertile [233  
U](/wiki/Uranium-233 "Uranium-233"), both of which emit strong [gamma rays](/wiki/Gamma_ray "Gamma ray"). As a result, the presence of these radionuclides complicates the radiation shielding of fresh nuclear fuel and the long-term storage and disposal of their spent nuclear fuel.[](#cite_note-WNA2024-Thorium-84)[](#cite_note-OECD2015-Thorium-85)

A 2022 study by Krall, Macfarlane and Ewing took a more critical approach, reporting that certain types of SMRs could produce more waste per unit of output power than conventional reactors—sometimes more than five times the amount of [spent nuclear fuel](/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel "Spent nuclear fuel") per kilowatt, and up to thirty-five times more waste generated by [neutron activation](/wiki/Neutron_activation "Neutron activation"), such as activated [steel](/wiki/Steel "Steel") and [graphite](/wiki/Graphite "Graphite").[](#cite_note-Barber2022-86)[](#cite_note-Vaughan2022-87)[](#cite_note-Stanford_University_2022-88)[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) The authors identified neutron leakage as a primary issue for SMRs, as these reactors have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio than conventional reactors. They calculated that, in smaller reactor cores, neutron leakage rates are significantly higher because emitted neutrons are less likely to interact with fissile atoms in the fuel and induce fission. Instead, more neutrons escape the core and are absorbed by materials used in [neutron reflectors](/wiki/Neutron_reflector "Neutron reflector") and shielding (thermal and gamma shields), rendering these materials radioactive waste through neutron activation.[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) Reactor designs using liquid metal coolants—such as molten sodium, lead, or lead-bismuth eutectic (LBE)—also become radioactive and contain activated impurities.[](#cite_note-WNA2024-LMC-89)

Another issue pinpointed by Krall _et al._ (2022) related to higher neutron leakage in SMRs is that a lower fraction of their nuclear fuel is consumed, resulting in lower [burnup](/wiki/Burnup "Burnup") and leaving more [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material") in their spent nuclear fuel, thereby increasing the waste volume. To sustain chain reactions in the smaller cores of SMRs, an alternative is to use nuclear fuel with a higher enrichment of [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235"). This could increase the risks of [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") and may require more stringent safeguard measures to prevent it (see also [IAEA safeguards](/wiki/IAEA_safeguards "IAEA safeguards")).[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52)

If higher concentrations of fissile material remain in the spent fuel, the [critical mass](/wiki/Critical_mass "Critical mass") needed to sustain a nuclear chain reaction is also lower. As a direct consequence, the number of spent fuel assemblies present in a waste canister must also be lower, necessitating a larger number of canisters and overpacks (containment structures) to avoid [criticality accidents](/wiki/Criticality_accident "Criticality accident") and guarantee [nuclear criticality safety](/wiki/Nuclear_criticality_safety "Nuclear criticality safety") in a deep geological repository.[](#cite_note-90) This also contributes to increased total waste volume and the number of disposal galleries needed in a geological repository.[](#cite_note-WNA2024-BackEnd-91)

Given the potential technical and economic importance of SMRs in providing zero-carbon electrical energy for [climate change mitigation](/wiki/Climate_change_mitigation "Climate change mitigation"), as well as the long-term and social relevance of managing and disposing of radioactive waste without imposing a negative burden on future generations, the publication of Krall _et al._ (2022) in the prestigious [PNAS](/wiki/PNAS "PNAS") journal has attracted numerous responses. These range from criticisms regarding the quality of their data and hypotheses[](#cite_note-NeutronBytes2022-92) to international debates on radioactive waste generated by SMRs and their decommissioning.[](#cite_note-NEA2022-93)

In an interview with François Diaz-Maurin, the [associate editor](/wiki/Associate_editor "Associate editor") of the [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_Atomic_Scientists "Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists"), Lindsay Krall—the lead author of the study and a former MacArthur postdoctoral fellow at Stanford's Center for International Security and Cooperation (CISAC)—addressed questions and criticisms, including those raised by the [NuScale](/wiki/NuScale "NuScale") reactor company.[](#cite_note-Diaz-Maurin2022-94) One of Krall's main concerns in the interview was: 

> There's definitely a disconnect between the people working on the back end of the fuel cycle—especially with geologic repository development—and those actually designing reactors. And, there is not a lot of motivation for these reactor designers to think about the geologic disposal aspects because the NRC's new reactor design certification application does not have a chapter on geologic disposal...[](#cite_note-Diaz-Maurin2022-94)

The high diversity of SMR reactors and their respective fuel cycles may also require more diverse waste management strategies to recycle or safely dispose of their nuclear waste.[](#cite_note-Barber2022-86)[](#cite_note-Krall2022-52) Managing a larger number of spent fuel types will be more challenging than the current situation, where most spent fuel comes from light water reactors. 

As Krall and Macfarlane stressed in a 2018 paper, some types of SMR spent fuels or coolants—such as highly reactive and corrosive [uranium fluoride](/wiki/Uranium_fluoride "Uranium fluoride") (UF4) from [molten salt reactors](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor "Molten salt reactor") or [pyrophoric](/wiki/Pyrophoric "Pyrophoric") sodium from liquid metal-cooled fast breeders—cannot be directly disposed of in a deep [geologic repository](/wiki/Geologic_repository "Geologic repository") because of their chemical reactivity in underground environments (such as deep [clay](/wiki/Clay "Clay") formations, crystalline rocks, or [rock salt](/wiki/Rock_salt "Rock salt")). To avoid exacerbating spent fuel storage and disposal issues, it will be necessary to reprocess and condition these materials in an appropriate and safe manner before final geological disposal.[](#cite_note-Krall_Macfarlane_2018-95)

A study by Keto _et al._ (2022) at the [VTT Technical Research Centre of Finland](/wiki/VTT_Technical_Research_Centre_of_Finland "VTT Technical Research Centre of Finland") also addressed the management of [spent nuclear fuel](/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel "Spent nuclear fuel") (SNF) and low- and intermediate-level waste (LILW) from the possible future deployment of SMRs in [Finland](/wiki/Finland "Finland"). The study indicated that, per gigawatt-electric-year (GWe-year), larger masses of SNF and other [high-level waste](/wiki/High-level_waste "High-level waste") (HLW), as well as larger volumes of [low-level waste](/wiki/Low-level_waste "Low-level waste") (LLW), would be produced by a light water SMR compared to a large nuclear power plant.[](#cite_note-Keto2022-96)

A report by the [German Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management](/wiki/Federal_Ministry_for_the_Environment,_Nature_Conservation,_Nuclear_Safety_and_Consumer_Protection "Federal Ministry for the Environment, Nature Conservation, Nuclear Safety and Consumer Protection") (BASE) found that extensive interim storage and fuel transports would still be required for SMRs. The report also concluded that a [deep geological repository](/wiki/Deep_geological_repository "Deep geological repository") is unavoidable due to the presence of highly mobile, long-lived fission products that cannot be efficiently [transmuted](/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation "Nuclear transmutation") because of their low [neutron cross section](/wiki/Neutron_cross_section "Neutron cross section"). This is the case with dose-dominating radionuclides such as [129  
I](/wiki/Iodine-129 "Iodine-129"), [99  
Tc](/wiki/Technetium-99 "Technetium-99"), and [79  
Se](/wiki/Selenium-79 "Selenium-79"), which exist as [soluble](/wiki/Soluble "Soluble") [anions](/wiki/Anion "Anion") that are not [sorbed](/wiki/Sorption "Sorption") onto the negatively charged minerals and are not retarded in geological media.[](#cite_note-base_report-41)

Nuclear waste is regulated within the existing nuclear governance systems, originally designed for conventional nuclear reactors. In the United States, oversight of waste is coordinated through the US Nuclear Regulatory Framework Commission and US Department of Energy. [](#cite_note-:4-97) SMRs produce broadly similar categories of waste as large reactors, however differences in deployment of scale, design, and use of SMR alters the transportation needs, logistics, and containment demands.[](#cite_note-:4-97)

## Nuclear proliferation

[Nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation"), or the use of nuclear materials to create weapons, is a concern for small modular reactors. As SMRs have lower generation capacity and are physically smaller, they are intended to be deployed in many more locations than conventional plants.[](#cite_note-auto1b2-98) SMRs are expected to substantially reduce staffing levels. The combination creates physical protection and security concerns.[](#cite_note-areva-20100618-99)[](#cite_note-nrc-201411052-61)

SMRs can be designed to use unconventional fuels allowing for higher [burnup](/wiki/Burnup "Burnup") and longer fuel cycles.[](#cite_note-:1-12) Longer refueling intervals could contribute to decrease the proliferation risks. Once the fuel has been [irradiated](/wiki/Irradiated "Irradiated"), the mixture of fission products and fissile materials is highly radioactive and requires special handling, preventing casual theft. 

Contrasting to conventional large reactors, SMRs can be adapted to be installed in a sealed underground chamber; therefore, "reducing the vulnerability of the reactor to a terrorist attack or a natural disaster".[](#cite_note-:14-73) New SMR designs enhance the proliferation resistance, such as those from the reactor design company Gen4. These models of SMR offer a solution capable of operating sealed underground for the life of the reactor following installation.[](#cite_note-:14-73)[](#cite_note-Ingersoll_2009_589–603-37)

Some SMR designs are designed for one-time fueling. This improves proliferation resistance by eliminating on-site nuclear fuel handling and means that the fuel can be sealed within the reactor. However, this design requires large amounts of fuel, which could make it a more attractive target. A 200 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe") 30-year core life light water SMR could contain about 2.5 tonnes of plutonium at end of life.[](#cite_note-nrc-201411052-61)

Furthermore, many SMRs offer the ability to go periods of greater than 10 years without requiring any form of refueling therefore improving the proliferation resistance as compared to conventional large reactors of which entail refueling every 18–24 months.[](#cite_note-:14-73)

Light-water reactors designed to run on [thorium](/wiki/Thorium "Thorium") offer increased [proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") resistance compared to the conventional uranium cycle, though [molten salt reactors](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactors "Molten salt reactors") have a substantial risk.[](#cite_note-U-232_and_the_proliferation-resistance_of_U-233_in_spent_fuel2-100)[](#cite_note-101)

SMRs are transported from the factories without fuel, as they are fueled on the ultimate site, except some [microreactors](/wiki/Nuclear_microreactor "Nuclear microreactor").[](#cite_note-102) This implies an independent transport of the fuel to the site and therefore increases the risk of nuclear proliferation. At the same time, millions of tons of nuclear waste are being shipped across the United States each year and there is no history of nuclear fuel or waste theft from these deliveries. 

## Licensing process

Licensing is an essential process required to guarantee the safety, security and safeguards of a new nuclear installation.[](#cite_note-3S_Risk_Analysis-103) As of 2025, only [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power")'s VOYGR, VOYGR-4, and VOYGR-6 SMRs are fully licensed for use in the United States.[](#cite_note-104) However, not all countries follow the NRC or IAEA licensing standards. In the United States and IAEA adhering countries, the licensing is based on a rigorous, independent analysis and reviewing work of all structures, systems and components critical for the nuclear safety under normal and accidental conditions on the whole service life of the installation including the long-term management of radioactive waste.[](#cite_note-CNSC2016-105) Licensing is based on the examination and scrutiny of the risk assessment studies and safety files elaborated by the fabricant and the exploitant of the SMR in the frame of the [safety case](/wiki/Safety_case "Safety case") they have to submit to the safety authority (regulatory body) when applying for a licence to construct and safely exploit the installation.[](#cite_note-Pistner2021-106) For NRC and IAEA licensing, the safety and feasibility cases of nuclear installations have to take into account all processes and elements important for the operational safety, its security (access protection), the nuclear safeguard (risk of proliferation), the proper conditioning of radioactive waste under a stable physico-chemical form, and the long-term safety related to the final disposal of the different types of radwaste produced, including all the waste produced during dismantling operations after decommissioning of the installation.[](#cite_note-CNSC2016-105)[](#cite_note-STUK2020-107)[](#cite_note-WENRA2021-108) A particularly important point of attention for the backend of the [nuclear fuel cycle](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel_cycle "Nuclear fuel cycle") is to avoid to producing poorly conditioned waste, or waste types without sustainable final destination or susceptible to generating unexpected reprocessing and disposal costs. 

The most common licensing process, applied by existing commercial reactors, is for the operation of light water reactors ([PWR](/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor "Pressurized water reactor") and [BWR](/wiki/Boiling_water_reactor "Boiling water reactor")). Early designs for large-scale reactors date back to the 1960s and 1970s during the construction of the nuclear reactor fleet currently in service. Some adaptations of the original licensing process by the US's [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") (NRC) have been repurposed to better correspond to the specific characteristics and needs of the deployment of SMR units.[](#cite_note-109) In particular, the US NRC process for [licensing](/wiki/Nuclear_licensing "Nuclear licensing") has focused mainly on conventional reactors. Design and safety specifications, human and organizational factors (including staffing requirements) have been developed for reactors with electrical output of more than 700 MWe.[](#cite_note-110)[](#cite_note-111)

To ensure adequate guidelines for the nuclear safety, while helping the licensing process, the IAEA has encouraged the creation of a central licensing system for SMRs.[](#cite_note-112) A workshop in October 2009 and another in June 2010 considered the topic, followed by an [US congressional hearing](/wiki/United_States_congressional_hearing "United States congressional hearing") in May 2010. 

The NRC and the [United States Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy") are working to define SMR licensing. The challenge of facilitating the development of SMRs is to prevent a weakening of the safety regulations: the risk of lightened regulations adopted more rapidly is to lower the safety characteristics of SMRs.[](#cite_note-113)[](#cite_note-smallBeauty-114)[](#cite_note-UCS2013-115) While deploying identical systems built in manufacturing plants with an improved quality control can be considered an advantage, SMRs remain nuclear reactors with a very high [energy density](/wiki/Energy_density "Energy density") and their smaller size is not _per se_ an intrinsic guarantee for a better safety. Any severe accident with external radioactive contamination release could have potential serious consequences not so different from that of a large LWR reactor. It would also probably signify the final rejection of [nuclear energy](/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power") by the public and the end of the [nuclear industry](/wiki/Nuclear_industry "Nuclear industry"). The potential "proliferation" of large SMR fleets and the high diversity of their design also complicate the licensing process. The nuclear safety cannot be sacrificed for industrial or economical interests and the risk of nuclear accident increases with the number of reactors in service, small or large unit. 

The US Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program was expected to help license and build two prototype SMRs during the 2020s, with up to $4 billion of government funding.[](#cite_note-science-202005202-116)

In July 2024, the [ADVANCE Act](/wiki/ADVANCE_Act "ADVANCE Act") directed the US NRC to develop a process to license and regulate microreactor designs. The Act is intended to expedite the deployment of microreactors, among other nuclear technologies.[](#cite_note-Goff-117)

## Flexibility

Small nuclear reactors, in comparison to conventional nuclear power plants, offer potential advantages related to the flexibility of their modular construction.[](#cite_note-:14-73) It would be possible to incrementally connect additional units to the grid in the event electrical load increases. Additionally, this flexibility in a standardized SMRs design revolving around modularity could allow for a faster production at a decreasing cost following the completion of the first reactor on site.[](#cite_note-:14-73)[](#cite_note-Ingersoll_2009_589–603-37)

The hypothesized flexibility and modularity of SMR is intended to allow additional power generation capability to be installed at existing power plants. A site could host several SMRs, one going off-line for refueling while the other reactors stay online as it is presently already the case for conventional larger reactors.[](#cite_note-:14-73)

SMRs operating in hybrid energy systems combining with renewables can develop multipurpose configurations developing system-level efficiency in sectors difficult to electrify.[](#cite_note-:5-118)

Hybrid system integration of SMRs without needed electrical generation foresees the direct use of thermal energy in co-generation. This includes desalination, district heating, industrial heating, industrial processes, and hydrogen production.[](#cite_note-119)

Flexibility of SMRs enable nuclear energy use within broader energy system integration, and expanding emission reduction goals to varying degrees. [](#cite_note-:5-118)

SMRs have been proposed for micro-grids in remote regions; flexibility of SMR allows for base-load generation, adjusting output from a response to demand. Complimenting wind and solar power intermittently.[](#cite_note-:6-120) SMRs enhancing resilience in geographically isolated communities.[](#cite_note-:6-120)

When electrical energy is not needed, some SMR designs foresee the direct use of thermal energy, minimizing so the energy loss. This includes "[desalination](/wiki/Desalination "Desalination"), industrial processes, [hydrogen production](/wiki/Hydrogen_production "Hydrogen production"), [shale oil recovery](/wiki/Shale_oil_extraction "Shale oil extraction"), and [district heating](/wiki/District_heating "District heating")", uses for which the present conventional larger reactors are not designed.[](#cite_note-:14-73)[](#cite_note-Cogeneration2-66)

## Economics

[](/wiki/File:Diagram_of_a_NuScale_reactor.jpg)A diagram of the early [NuScale](/wiki/NuScale "NuScale") Power Module reactor (50 MWe) in 2022.[](#cite_note-121) The module was the first in the US to be NRC approved. NuScale upgraded the Power Module output to 77 MWe and received NRC approval for its use in the VOYGR-4 (308 MWe) and the VOYGR-6 (462 MWe) in May 2025.[](#cite_note-122)

A SMR factory would require substantial upfront capital. Per-unit costs would only become economical when an estimated 40–70 units are produced.[](#cite_note-123)[](#cite_note-sciencedirect.com-124)

Another potential advantage is that a future power station using SMRs can begin with a single module and expand by adding modules as demand grows. This reduces startup costs associated with conventional designs.[](#cite_note-Mignacca2-54) Some SMRs also have a [load-following](/wiki/Load-following "Load-following") design such that they could produce less electricity when demand is low. 

According to a 2014 study of electricity production in decentralized microgrids, the total cost of using SMRs for electricity generation would be significantly lower compared to the total cost of [offshore wind power](/wiki/Offshore_wind_power "Offshore wind power"), [solar thermal energy](/wiki/Solar_thermal_energy "Solar thermal energy"), [biomass](/wiki/Biomass_\(energy\) "Biomass \(energy\)"), and [solar photovoltaic](/wiki/Solar_photovoltaic "Solar photovoltaic") electricity generation plants.[](#cite_note-:03-125)

Construction costs per SMR reactor were claimed in 2016 to be less than that for a conventional nuclear plant, while exploitation costs might be higher for SMRs due to low scale economics and the higher number of reactors. SMR staff operating costs per unit output can be as much as 190% higher than the fixed operating cost of fewer large reactors.[](#cite_note-eysmr-2016032-126) Modular building is a very complex process and there is "extremely limited information about SMR modules transportation", according to a 2019 report.[](#cite_note-transportation2-42)

A production cost calculation done by the German Federal Office for the Safety of Nuclear Waste Management (BASE), taking into account economies of scale and learning effects from the nuclear industry, suggests that an average of 3,000 SMR would have to be produced before SMR production would be worthwhile. This is because the construction costs of SMRs are relatively higher than those of large nuclear power plants due to the low electrical output.[](#cite_note-base_report-41)

In 2017, an Energy Innovation Reform Project (EIRP) study of eight companies looked at reactor designs with capacity between 47.5 MWe and 1,648 MWe.[](#cite_note-127) The study reported average capital cost of $3,782/kW, average operating cost total of $21/MWh and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) of $60/MWh. 

In 2020, [Energy Impact Center](/wiki/Energy_Impact_Center "Energy Impact Center") founder Bret Kugelmass claimed that thousands of SMRs could be built in parallel, "thus reducing costs associated with long borrowing times for prolonged construction schedules and reducing risk premiums currently linked to large projects".[](#cite_note-reutersevents.com2-128) [GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy](/wiki/GE_Vernova_Hitachi_Nuclear_Energy "GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy") Executive Vice President Jon Ball agreed, saying the modular elements of SMRs would also help reduce costs associated with extended construction times.[](#cite_note-reutersevents.com2-128)

In October 2023, an academic paper published in _[Energy](/wiki/Energy_\(journal\) "Energy \(journal\)")_ collated the basic economic data of 19 more developed SMR designs, and modeled their costs in a consistent manner. A [Monte Carlo simulation](/wiki/Monte_Carlo_simulation "Monte Carlo simulation") showed that none were profitable or economically competitive. For the closer to market PWR SMRs the median LCOEs ranged from $218/MWh to $614/MWh (in 2020 US dollars), with lower [first quartile](/wiki/First_quartile "First quartile") estimates from $188/MWh to $385/MWh. The three [high-temperature gas-cooled reactor](/wiki/High-temperature_gas-cooled_reactor "High-temperature gas-cooled reactor") designs, which needed more development time, had lower median LCOEs from $116/MWh to $137/MWh.[](#cite_note-steigerwald-20231015-2)

The first SMR deployment project in the US was the [Carbon Free Power Project](/wiki/Carbon_Free_Power_Project "Carbon Free Power Project"), which planned to deploy six 77 MWe [NuScale](/wiki/NuScale "NuScale") reactors, reduced from twelve in earlier plans. Estimated target electricity generation price after subsidies was $89/MWh in 2023, an increase from $58/MWh in 2021. The increased generation cost led to the decision to cancel the project in November 2023.[](#cite_note-eenews-20231109-129) Before its cancellation, the project received a $1.355 billion cost-share award toward construction costs from the US government in 2020[](#cite_note-wnn-20201019-130) plus an estimated $30/MWh generation subsidy from the [Inflation Reduction Act of 2022](/wiki/Inflation_Reduction_Act_of_2022 "Inflation Reduction Act of 2022").[](#cite_note-wnn-20230109-131) Unsubsidized cost estimates at cancellation were a capital cost of $20,139/kW and generating cost of $119/MWe.[](#cite_note-ieefa-20230111-132) This raised concerns about the commercial prospects in the US of the other SMR designs.[](#cite_note-science-20231110-133)

In 2024, Australian scientific research body [CSIRO](/wiki/CSIRO "CSIRO") estimated that electricity produced in Australia by a SMR constructed from 2023 would cost roughly 2.5 times that produced by a traditional large nuclear plant, falling to about 1.6 times by 2030.[](#cite_note-wnn-20240522-134)[](#cite_note-csiro-202405-135)

The final investment decision in 2025 to proceed with the build of a [BWRX-300](/wiki/BWRX-300 "BWRX-300") SMR in Canada was based on a forecast cost of [CA$](/wiki/CA$ "CA$")7.7 billion (US$5.6 billion), with an estimated cost of CA$13.2 billion (US$9.6 billion) for three further units. These costs include finance charges and some contingency.[](#cite_note-wnn-20250523-136)

## Siting/infrastructure

SMRs are expected to require less land, e.g., the 470 MWe 3-loop [Rolls-Royce SMR](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_SMR "Rolls-Royce SMR") reactor should take 40,000 m2 (430,000 sq ft), 10% of that needed for a traditional plant.[](#cite_note-rollsroyce-smr-brochure-2017-210) This unit is too large to meet the [International Atomic Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency "International Atomic Energy Agency")'s definition of a SMR being smaller than 300MWe[](#cite_note-iaea-smr-definition-211) and will require more on-site construction, which calls into question the claimed benefits of SMRs. The firm is targeting a 500-day construction time.[](#cite_note-rollsroyce-2017-212)

Electricity needs in remote locations are usually small and variable, making them suitable for a smaller plant.[](#cite_note-Report_to_Congress-38): 8 The smaller size may also reduce the need to access to a large grid to distribute their output. 

## Proposed sites

### Argentina

In February 2014, the [CAREM](/wiki/CAREM "CAREM") SMR project started in [Argentina](/wiki/Argentina "Argentina") with the civil engineering construction of the [containment building](/wiki/Containment_building "Containment building") of a prototype reactor. The CAREM acronym means _Central ARgentina de Elementos Modulares_. The [National Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/National_Atomic_Energy_Commission "National Atomic Energy Commission") ([Spanish](/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language"): _Comisión Nacional de Energía Atómica_ , CNEA), the [Argentine](/wiki/Argentina "Argentina") [government](/wiki/Government_of_Argentina "Government of Argentina") agency in charge of nuclear energy research and development and [Nucleoeléctrica Argentina](/w/index.php?title=Nucleoel%C3%A9ctrica_Argentina&action=edit&redlink=1 "Nucleoeléctrica Argentina \(page does not exist\)") [[es](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleoelectrica_Argentina_SA "es:Nucleoelectrica Argentina SA")], the national nuclear energy company, are cooperating to achieve the realization of the project.[](#cite_note-WorldNuclearNews2023-213)

CAREM-25 is a prototype of 25 MWe, the first nuclear power plant completely designed and developed in Argentina.[](#cite_note-WorldNuclearNews2023-213) The project was suspended several times before being resumed. In October 2022, CNEA expected that the civil construction works would be finished by 2024. If construction continues according to plan, the first criticality of CAREM-25 is foreseen by the end of 2027.[](#cite_note-WorldNuclearNews2023-213)

### Canada

In 2018, the Canadian province of [New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick "New Brunswick") announced it would invest $10 million for a demonstration project at the [Point Lepreau Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Point_Lepreau_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Point Lepreau Nuclear Generating Station").[](#cite_note-214) It was later announced that SMR proponents Advanced Reactor Concepts[](#cite_note-215) and Moltex[](#cite_note-216) would open offices there. One unit is scheduled for construction at Point Lepreau Nuclear Generating Station, Canada, in July 2018. Both Moltex and ARC Nuclear are vying for the contract.[](#cite_note-217)

On 1 December 2019, the Premiers of [Ontario](/wiki/Ontario "Ontario"), New Brunswick and [Saskatchewan](/wiki/Saskatchewan "Saskatchewan") signed a [memorandum of understanding](/wiki/Memorandum_of_understanding "Memorandum of understanding") (MoU) [](#cite_note-218) "committing to collaborate on the development and deployment of innovative, versatile and scalable nuclear reactors, known as Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)."[](#cite_note-219) They were joined by [Alberta](/wiki/Alberta "Alberta") in August 2020.[](#cite_note-220) Continued support from citizens and government officials has led to the execution of a selected SMR at the Canadian Nuclear Laboratory.[](#cite_note-:24-6)

In 2021, [Ontario Power Generation](/wiki/Ontario_Power_Generation "Ontario Power Generation") announced they plan to build a [BWRX-300](/wiki/BWRX-300 "BWRX-300") SMR at their [Darlington site](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station") to be completed by 2028. A licence for construction still had to be applied for.[](#cite_note-221)

On 11 August 2022, Invest Alberta, the Government of Alberta's crown corporation signed a MoU with [Terrestrial Energy](/wiki/Terrestrial_Energy "Terrestrial Energy") regarding [IMSR](/wiki/IMSR "IMSR") in Western Canada through an interprovincial MoU it joined earlier.[](#cite_note-222)

### China

In July 2019, [China National Nuclear Corporation](/wiki/China_National_Nuclear_Corporation "China National Nuclear Corporation") (CNNC) announced it would build an [ACP100](/wiki/ACP100 "ACP100") SMR on the north-west side of the existing [Changjiang Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Changjiang_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Changjiang Nuclear Power Plant") at [Changjiang](/wiki/Changjiang "Changjiang"), in the [Hainan](/wiki/Hainan "Hainan") province by the end of the year.[](#cite_note-wnn-20190722-223) On 7 June 2021, the demonstration project, named the [Linglong One](/wiki/Linglong_One "Linglong One"), was approved by China's National Development and Reform Commission.[](#cite_note-224) In July, CNNC started construction,[](#cite_note-225) and in October 2021, the containment vessel bottom of the first of two units was installed. It is the world's first commercial land-based SMR prototype.[](#cite_note-containment-linglong1-56)

In August 2023, the core module was installed. The core module includes an integrated [pressure vessel](/wiki/Pressure_vessel "Pressure vessel"), [steam generator](/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\) "Steam generator \(nuclear power\)"), primary pump receiver. The reactor's planned capacity is 125 MWe.[](#cite_note-226)

### France

At the beginning of 2023, [Électricité de France](/wiki/%C3%89lectricit%C3%A9_de_France "Électricité de France") (EDF) created a new subsidiary to develop and construct a new SMR named Nuward. It was a 340 MWe design with two independent light water reactors of 170 MWe. The twin reactors were sheltered in a single containment building sharing most of their equipment.[](#cite_note-EDF2022-227) In August 2023, EDF submitted a [safety case](/wiki/Safety_case "Safety case") for Nuward to the _[autorité de sûreté nucléaire](/wiki/Autorit%C3%A9_de_s%C3%BBret%C3%A9_nucl%C3%A9aire "Autorité de sûreté nucléaire")_ (ASN), the French safety authority.[](#cite_note-Lopez2023-228)

In July 2024, EDF announced it was discontinuing the existing design process for Nuward, and will work on an SMR design based on existing rather than innovative technologies, following discussions with prospective SMR customers.[](#cite_note-nei-20240704-173)[](#cite_note-nei-20240711-174) In January 2025, EDF announced that the new Nuward conceptual design would be completed by mid-2026 to come to market in the 2030s, with an output of about 400 MWe and usable heat output of 100 MWt.[](#cite_note-wnn-20250107-229)

### Poland

Polish chemical company Synthos declared plans to deploy a [Hitachi](/wiki/Hitachi "Hitachi") [BWRX-300](/wiki/BWRX-300 "BWRX-300") reactor (300 MW) in Poland by 2030.[](#cite_note-230) A feasibility study was completed in December 2020 and the licensing process started with the Polish National Atomic Energy Agency.[](#cite_note-231)

In February 2022, [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power") and the large mining [conglomerate](/wiki/Conglomerate_\(company\) "Conglomerate \(company\)") [KGHM Polska Miedź](/wiki/KGHM_Polska_Mied%C5%BA "KGHM Polska Miedź") announced signing of contract to construct a first operational reactor in Poland by 2029.[](#cite_note-232)

### Romania

On the occasion of [2021 United Nations Climate Change Conference](/wiki/2021_United_Nations_Climate_Change_Conference "2021 United Nations Climate Change Conference"), the state-owned Romanian nuclear energy company [Nuclearelectrica](/wiki/Nuclearelectrica "Nuclearelectrica") and [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power") signed an agreement to build a power plant with six small-scale nuclear reactors at the [Doicești power station](/wiki/Doice%C8%99ti_Power_Station "Doicești Power Station"), on the site of a former [coal power plant](/wiki/Coal_power_plant "Coal power plant"), located near the village of [Doicești](/wiki/Doice%C8%99ti "Doicești"), [Dâmbovița county](/wiki/D%C3%A2mbovi%C8%9Ba_County "Dâmbovița County"), 90 km North of [Bucharest](/wiki/Bucharest "Bucharest"). The project is estimated to be completed by 2026–2027, which will make the power plant the first of its kind in Europe. The power plant is expected to generate 462 MWe, securing the consumption of about 46.000 households and would help to avoid the release of 4 million tons of CO2 per year.[](#cite_note-233)[](#cite_note-234)[](#cite_note-235)

### Russia

Russia has started to deploy on its [arctic coast](/wiki/Arctic_coast "Arctic coast") small nuclear reactors embarked on board [icebreakers](/wiki/Icebreaker "Icebreaker"). In May 2020, the first prototype of a [floating nuclear power plant](/wiki/Floating_nuclear_power_plant "Floating nuclear power plant") with two 30 MWe reactors – the type _[KLT-40](/wiki/KLT-40 "KLT-40")_ – started operation in [Pevek](/wiki/Pevek "Pevek"), Russia.[](#cite_note-klt-40_pris-20) This concept is based on the design of nuclear icebreakers.[](#cite_note-klt40-55)

### United Kingdom

In 2016, it was reported that the [UK Government](/wiki/UK_Government "UK Government") was assessing [Welsh](/wiki/Wales "Wales") SMR sites – including the former [Trawsfynydd nuclear power station](/wiki/Trawsfynydd_nuclear_power_station "Trawsfynydd nuclear power station") – and on the site of former nuclear or [coal-fired power stations](/wiki/Coal-fired_power_station "Coal-fired power station") in [Northern England](/wiki/Northern_England "Northern England"). Existing nuclear sites including [Bradwell](/wiki/Bradwell_nuclear_power_station "Bradwell nuclear power station"), [Hartlepool](/wiki/Hartlepool_Nuclear_Power_Station "Hartlepool Nuclear Power Station"), [Heysham](/wiki/Heysham_nuclear_power_station "Heysham nuclear power station"), [Oldbury](/wiki/Oldbury_Nuclear_Power_Station "Oldbury Nuclear Power Station"), [Sizewell](/wiki/Sizewell_nuclear_power_stations "Sizewell nuclear power stations"), [Sellafield](/wiki/Sellafield "Sellafield"), and [Wylfa](/wiki/Wylfa "Wylfa") were stated to be possibilities.[](#cite_note-236) The target cost for a 470 MWe [Rolls-Royce SMR](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_SMR "Rolls-Royce SMR") unit is £1.8 billion for the fifth unit built.[](#cite_note-wnn-20191107-237)[](#cite_note-rr-20210908-238) In 2020, it was reported that [Rolls-Royce](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_Holdings "Rolls-Royce Holdings") had plans to construct up to 16 SMRs in the UK. In 2019, the company received £18 million to begin designing the modular system.[](#cite_note-239) An additional £210 million was awarded to Rolls-Royce by the British government in 2021, complemented by a £195 million contribution from private firms.[](#cite_note-240) In November 2022, Rolls-Royce announced that the sites at [Trawsfynydd](/wiki/Trawsfynydd "Trawsfynydd"), Wylfa, Sellafield and Oldbury would be prioritised for assessment as potential locations for multiple SMRs.[](#cite_note-241)

The British government launched [Great British Nuclear](/wiki/Great_British_Nuclear "Great British Nuclear") in July 2023 to administer a competition to create SMRs, and will co-fund any viable project.[](#cite_note-BBCJul23-242)

### United States

Researchers at [Oregon State University](/wiki/Oregon_State_University "Oregon State University") (OSU), headed by José N. Reyes Jr., designed a prototype SMR in 2007.[](#cite_note-243) Their research and design component prototypes formed the basis for [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power")'s commercial SMR design. NuScale and OSU developed the first full-scale SMR prototype in 2013[](#cite_note-244) and NuScale received the first [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") Design Certification approval for a commercial SMR in the United States in 2022.[](#cite_note-245) In 2025, two more NuScale SMRs, the VOYGR-4 and VOYGR-6, received NRC approval.[](#cite_note-246) The [US Department of Energy](/wiki/US_Department_of_Energy "US Department of Energy") had estimated the first SMR in the United States would be completed by [NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power") around 2030,[](#cite_note-247) but this deal has since fallen through after the customers backed out due to rising costs.[](#cite_note-science-20231110-133)[](#cite_note-CNBC-18) In 2024, the US had nearly 4 gigawatts in announced SMR projects in addition to almost 3 GW in early development or pre-development stages, according to Utility Dive.[](#cite_note-UD-SMR-248)

SMRs differ in terms of staffing, safety and deployment time.[](#cite_note-249) US government studies to evaluate SMR-associated risks are claimed to have slowed the licensing process.[](#cite_note-smallBeauty-114)[](#cite_note-sciencedirect.com-124) One main concern with SMRs and their large number, needed to reach an economic profitability, is preventing [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation").[](#cite_note-areva-20100618-99)[](#cite_note-auto1b-250)

[](/wiki/File:Former_Genoa_coal_power_plant.jpg "Former Genoa coal power plant and dry cask storage, July 2023. The La Crosse BWR is not in these photos since it was demolished in 2019.")

[ ](/wiki/File:Former_Genoa_coal_power_plant.jpg "File:Former Genoa coal power plant.jpg")

Former [Genoa](/wiki/Genoa,_Wisconsin "Genoa, Wisconsin") coal power plant and dry cask storage, July 2023. The La Crosse [BWR](/wiki/La_Crosse_Boiling_Water_Reactor "La Crosse Boiling Water Reactor") is not in these photos since it was demolished in 2019.

[NuScale Power](/wiki/NuScale_Power "NuScale Power") is working with Wisconsin's Dairyland Power to evaluate VOYGR SMR power plants for potential deployment. The US leader in SMR technology believes its load-following capabilities can be used to support Dairyland's existing renewables portfolio, as well as facilitate growth. Additionally, VOYGR plants are well-suited for replacing Dairyland's retiring coal plant sites, preserving critical jobs and helping communities transition to a decarbonized energy system.[](#cite_note-251)

The [Tennessee Valley Authority](/wiki/Tennessee_Valley_Authority "Tennessee Valley Authority") was authorized to receive an Early Site Permit (ESP) by the [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") for siting an SMR at its [Clinch River Nuclear Site](/wiki/Clinch_River_Nuclear_Site "Clinch River Nuclear Site") in [Tennessee](/wiki/Tennessee "Tennessee") in December 2019.[](#cite_note-252) This ESP is valid for 20 years, and addresses site safety, environmental protection and emergency preparedness. This ESP is applicable for any [light-water reactor](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor") SMR design under development in the United States.[](#cite_note-253) In 2025, the TVA contracted [Entra1 Energy](/w/index.php?title=Entra1_Energy&action=edit&redlink=1 "Entra1 Energy \(page does not exist\)") to manage the building of a 6-Gigawatt NuScale Power SMR plant on TVA's site.[](#cite_note-254)

NuScale Power is working with Associated Electric Cooperative Inc. (Associated) in Missouri to evaluate deployment of VOYGR SMR power plants as part of Associated's due diligence to explore reliable, responsible sources of energy.[](#cite_note-255) Their design of SMRs are intended to operate uninterrupted, with LEU refueling required every 2 years. Some of their SMR modules can be moved into a separate refueling pool while the remaining modules remain active.[](#cite_note-256)

The Utah Associated Municipal Power Systems (UAMPS) had partnered with [Energy Northwest](/wiki/Energy_Northwest "Energy Northwest") to explore siting a NuScale Power reactor in [Idaho](/wiki/Idaho "Idaho"), possibly on the Department of Energy's [Idaho National Laboratory](/wiki/Idaho_National_Laboratory "Idaho National Laboratory").[](#cite_note-257)[](#cite_note-eenews-20231109-129) Known as the [Carbon Free Power Project](/wiki/Carbon_Free_Power_Project "Carbon Free Power Project"), the project was canceled in November 2023 for cost reasons.[](#cite_note-eenews-20231109-129) NuScale said in January 2023 the target price for power from the plant was $89 per megawatt hour, up 53% from the previous estimate of $58 per MWh, raising concerns about customers' willingness to pay.[](#cite_note-258)

The [Galena Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Galena_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Galena Nuclear Power Plant") in [Galena, Alaska](/wiki/Galena,_Alaska "Galena, Alaska"), was a proposed micro nuclear reactor installation. It was a potential deployment for the [Toshiba 4S](/wiki/Toshiba_4S "Toshiba 4S") reactor.[](#cite_note-259) The project was "effectively stalled". Toshiba never began the expensive process for approval that is required by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission. 

In October 2024, [Google](/wiki/Google "Google") agreed to commission multiple small modular reactors from [Kairos Power](/wiki/Kairos_Power "Kairos Power") to power its [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") processing, with the first to be operational in 2030.[](#cite_note-DeGeurin_2024-260)[](#cite_note-Silva_2024-261)[](#cite_note-Terrell_2024-262)

In December 2025, the Department of Energy selected [Tennessee Valley Authority](/wiki/Tennessee_Valley_Authority "Tennessee Valley Authority") and [Holtec](/wiki/Holtec "Holtec") to receive grants of $400 million each to support early deployment of advanced light-water SMRs, with an SMR defined as having output of between 50 and 350 MWe.[](#cite_note-wnn-20251203-263)

In 2026, Antares Nuclear announced that its reactor had achieved criticality, claiming to be the first privately developed, non-light-water reactor to do so.[](#cite_note-264)
