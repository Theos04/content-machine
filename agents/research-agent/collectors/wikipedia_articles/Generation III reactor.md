Class of nuclear reactors with improved safety over its predecessors

**Generation III reactors** , or **Gen III reactors** , are a class of [nuclear reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") designed to succeed [Generation II](/wiki/Generation_II_reactor "Generation II reactor") reactors, incorporating evolutionary improvements in design. These include improved [fuel technology](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel "Nuclear fuel"), higher [thermal efficiency](/wiki/Thermal_efficiency "Thermal efficiency"), significantly enhanced safety systems (including [passive nuclear safety](/wiki/Passive_nuclear_safety "Passive nuclear safety")), and standardized designs intended to reduce maintenance and capital costs. They are promoted by the [Generation IV International Forum](/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor#Generation_IV_International_Forum "Generation IV reactor") (GIF). 

The first Generation III reactors to begin operation were [Kashiwazaki 6 and 7](/wiki/Kashiwazaki-Kariwa_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Nuclear Power Plant") [advanced boiling water reactors](/wiki/Advanced_boiling_water_reactor "Advanced boiling water reactor") (ABWRs) in 1996 and 1997. From 2012, both have been shut down due to a less permissive political environment in the wake of the [Fukushima nuclear accident](/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_accident "Fukushima nuclear accident"). Due to the prolonged period of stagnation in the construction of new reactors and the continued (albeit declining) popularity of Generation II/II+ designs in new construction, relatively few third generation reactors have been built. 

## Overview

The older Gen II reactors comprise the vast majority of current nuclear reactors. Gen III reactors are so-called advanced [light-water reactors](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor") (LWRs). Gen III+ reactors are labeled as "evolutionary designs". Though the distinction between Gen II and III reactors is arbitrary, few Gen III reactors have reached the commercial stage as of 2022. The [Generation IV International Forum](/wiki/Generation_IV_International_Forum "Generation IV International Forum") calls Gen IV reactors "revolutionary designs". These are concepts for which no concrete prognoses for realization existed at the time.[](#cite_note-1)

The improvements in reactor technology in third generation reactors are intended to result in a longer operational life (designed for 60 years of operation, extendable to 100+ years of operation prior to complete overhaul and [reactor pressure vessel](/wiki/Reactor_pressure_vessel "Reactor pressure vessel") replacement) compared with currently used Generation II reactors (designed for 40 years of operation, extendable to 60+ years of operation prior to complete overhaul and pressure vessel replacement).[](#cite_note-2)[](#cite_note-3)

The [core damage frequencies](/wiki/Core_damage_frequency "Core damage frequency") for these reactors are designed to be lower than for Generation II reactors – 60 core damage events for the [European Pressurized Reactor](/wiki/European_Pressurized_Reactor "European Pressurized Reactor") (EPR) and 3 core damage events for the [Economic Simplified Boiling Water Reactor](/wiki/Economic_Simplified_Boiling_Water_Reactor "Economic Simplified Boiling Water Reactor") (ESBWR)[](#cite_note-ansESBWR-4) per 100 million reactor-years are significantly lower than the 1,000 core damage events per 100 million reactor-years for BWR/4 Generation II reactors.[](#cite_note-ansESBWR-4)

The third generation EPR reactor was also designed to use [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium") more efficiently than older Generation II reactors, using approximately 17% less per unit of electricity generated than these older reactor technologies.[](#cite_note-5) An independent analysis conducted by environmental scientist [Barry Brook](/wiki/Barry_Brook_\(scientist\) "Barry Brook \(scientist\)") on the greater efficiency and therefore lower material needs of Gen III reactors, corroborates this finding.[](#cite_note-6)

## Developments

[](/wiki/File:Schemata_core_catcher_EPR.jpg)EPR [core catching room](/wiki/Core_catcher "Core catcher") designed to catch the [corium](/wiki/Corium_\(nuclear_reactor\) "Corium \(nuclear reactor\)") in case of a [meltdown](/wiki/Nuclear_meltdown "Nuclear meltdown"). Some Generation III reactors include a core catcher in their design.

Gen III+ reactor designs are an evolutionary development of Gen III reactors, offering improvements in safety over Gen III reactor designs. Manufacturers began development of Gen III+ systems in the 1990s by building on the operating experience of the American, Japanese, and Western European [light-water reactor](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The nuclear industry began to promote a [nuclear renaissance](/wiki/Nuclear_renaissance "Nuclear renaissance") suggesting that Gen III+ designs should solve three key problems: safety, cost and buildability. Construction costs of US$1,000/kW were forecast, a level that would make nuclear competitive with gas, and construction times of four years or less were expected. However, these estimates proved over-optimistic.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

A notable improvement of Gen III+ systems over second-generation designs is the incorporation in some designs of passive safety features that do not require active controls or operator intervention but instead rely on gravity or natural convection to mitigate the impact of abnormal events.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

[](/wiki/File:PHWR_under_Construction_at_Kakrapar_Gujarat_India.jpg)[Kakrapar Atomic Power Station](/wiki/Kakrapar_Atomic_Power_Station "Kakrapar Atomic Power Station") Unit 3 and 4 under construction. India's first Generation III+ reactor

Generation III+ reactors incorporate extra safety features to avoid the kind of disaster suffered at [Fukushima](/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster "Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster") in 2011. Generation III+ designs, passive safety, also known as passive cooling, requires no sustained operator action or electronic feedback to shut down the plant safely in the event of an emergency. Many of the Generation III+ nuclear reactors have a [core catcher](/wiki/Core_catcher "Core catcher"). If the fuel cladding and reactor vessel systems and associated piping become molten, [corium](/wiki/Corium_\(nuclear_reactor\) "Corium \(nuclear reactor\)") will fall into a core catcher which holds the molten material and has the ability to cool it. This, in turn protects the final barrier, the [containment building](/wiki/Containment_building "Containment building"). As an example, [Rosatom](/wiki/Rosatom "Rosatom") installed a 200-tonne core catcher in the [VVER](/wiki/VVER-1200 "VVER-1200") reactor as the first large piece of equipment in the reactor building of [Rooppur 1](/wiki/Rooppur_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant"), describing it as "a unique protection system".[](#cite_note-7)[](#cite_note-8) In 2017, Rosatom has started commercial operations of the [NVNPP-2](/wiki/Novovoronezh_Nuclear_Power_Plant_II "Novovoronezh Nuclear Power Plant II") Unit 1 [VVER-1200](/wiki/VVER "VVER") reactor in central Russia, marking the world's first full start-up of a generation III+ reactor.[](#cite_note-9)

### First reactors

[](/wiki/File:Usina_Nuclear_em_Novovoronezh,_R%C3%BAssia_01.jpg)[Novovoronezh Nuclear Power Plant II](/wiki/Novovoronezh_Nuclear_Power_Plant_II "Novovoronezh Nuclear Power Plant II") with the first Generation III+ nuclear reactor in the world

The first Generation III reactors were built in Japan, in the form of [advanced boiling water reactors](/wiki/Advanced_boiling_water_reactor "Advanced boiling water reactor"). On 5 August 2016, a Generation III+ [VVER-1200](/wiki/VVER-1200 "VVER-1200")/392M reactor became operational (first grid connection) at [Novovoronezh Nuclear Power Plant II](/wiki/Novovoronezh_Nuclear_Power_Plant_II "Novovoronezh Nuclear Power Plant II") in Russia,[](#cite_note-10) which was the first operational Generation III+ reactor.[](#cite_note-11)

Several other Generation III+ reactors are under late-stage construction in Europe, China, India, and the United States. The next Generation III+ reactors to come online were an [AREVA](/wiki/AREVA "AREVA") [EPR](/wiki/EPR_\(nuclear_reactor\) "EPR \(nuclear reactor\)") reactor at the [Taishan Nuclear Power Station](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant") (first grid connection on 2018-06-29) and a Westinghouse [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") reactor at the [Sanmen Nuclear Power Station](/wiki/Sanmen_Nuclear_Power_Station "Sanmen Nuclear Power Station") (first grid connection on 2018-06-30) in China.[](#cite_note-12)

In the United States, reactor designs are certified by the [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") (NRC). As of August 2020[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Generation_III_reactor&action=edit), the commission has approved seven new designs, and is considering one more design as well as renewal of an expired certification.[](#cite_note-NRC-DCANR-13)

## Response and criticism

Proponents of nuclear power and some who have historically been critical have acknowledged that third generation reactors as a whole are safer than older reactors.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

[Edwin Lyman](/wiki/Edwin_Lyman "Edwin Lyman"), a senior staff scientist at the [Union of Concerned Scientists](/wiki/Union_of_Concerned_Scientists "Union of Concerned Scientists"), has challenged specific cost-saving design choices made for two Generation III reactors, both the [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") and [ESBWR](/wiki/ESBWR "ESBWR"). Lyman, John Ma (a senior structural engineer at the NRC), and [Arnold Gundersen](/wiki/Arnold_Gundersen "Arnold Gundersen") (an [anti-nuclear](/wiki/Anti-nuclear "Anti-nuclear") consultant) are concerned about what they perceive as weaknesses in the steel containment vessel and the concrete shield building around the AP1000 in that its containment vessel does not have sufficient safety margins in the event of a direct airplane strike.[](#cite_note-bs11-14)[](#cite_note-mlw-15) Other engineers do not agree with these concerns, and claim the containment building is more than sufficient in safety margins and [factors of safety](/wiki/Factor_of_safety "Factor of safety").[](#cite_note-mlw-15)[](#cite_note-nytimes.com-16)

The [Union of Concerned Scientists](/wiki/Union_of_Concerned_Scientists "Union of Concerned Scientists") in 2008 referred to the [EPR](/wiki/European_Pressurized_Reactor "European Pressurized Reactor") as the only new reactor design under consideration in the United States that "...appears to have the potential to be significantly safer and more secure against attack than today's reactors."[](#cite_note-ucs-npww-17): 7

There have also been issues in fabricating the precision parts necessary to maintain safe operation of these reactors, with cost overruns, broken parts, and extremely fine steel tolerances causing issues with new reactors under construction in [France](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France "Nuclear power in France") at the [Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Flamanville_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant").[](#cite_note-18)
