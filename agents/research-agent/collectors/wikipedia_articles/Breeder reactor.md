Nuclear reactor generating more fissile material than it consumes

[](/wiki/File:%D0%91%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%BE%D1%8F%D1%80%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%8F_%D0%90%D0%AD%D0%A1_2019_%D0%B3%D0%BE%D0%B4.jpg)The [Beloyarsk Nuclear Power Station](/wiki/Beloyarsk_Nuclear_Power_Station "Beloyarsk Nuclear Power Station"), site of the [BN-600](/wiki/BN-600 "BN-600") and [BN-800](/wiki/BN-800 "BN-800"), the only two commercially operational breeder reactors as of 2026

A **breeder reactor** is a [nuclear reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") that generates more [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material") than it consumes.[](#cite_note-Waltar-1) These reactors can be [fueled](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel "Nuclear fuel") with more-commonly available [isotopes of uranium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_uranium "Isotopes of uranium") and [thorium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_thorium "Isotopes of thorium"), such as [uranium-238](/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238") and [thorium-232](/wiki/Thorium-232 "Thorium-232"), as opposed to the rare [uranium-235](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") which is used in conventional reactors. These materials are called [fertile materials](/wiki/Fertile_material "Fertile material") since they can be bred into fuel by these breeder reactors. 

Breeder reactors achieve this because their [neutron economy](/wiki/Neutron_economy "Neutron economy") is high enough to create more fissile fuel than they use. These extra neutrons are absorbed by the fertile material that is loaded into the reactor along with fissile fuel. This [irradiated](/wiki/Irradiated "Irradiated") fertile material in turn transmutes into fissile material which can undergo [fission reactions](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission"). 

Breeders were at first found attractive because they made more complete use of uranium fuel than [light-water reactors](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor"), but interest declined after the 1960s as more uranium reserves were found[](#cite_note-Helmreich-2) and new methods of [uranium enrichment](/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment") reduced fuel costs. 

Breeder reactors have been developed and operated in Russia, India, Japan, the United States, France, and China, but only Russia is currently operating a commercial fast breeder reactor as of April 2026. 

## Types

[](/wiki/File:Sasahara.svg)Production of heavy transuranic actinides in current thermal-neutron fission reactors through neutron capture and decays. Starting at uranium-238, isotopes of plutonium, americium, and curium are all produced. In a fast neutron-breeder reactor, all these isotopes may be burned as fuel.

Many types of breeder reactor are possible: 

A "breeder" is simply a [nuclear reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") designed for very high [neutron economy](/wiki/Neutron_economy "Neutron economy") with an associated conversion rate higher than 1.0. In principle, almost any reactor design could be tweaked to become a breeder. For example, the [light-water reactor](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor"), a heavily moderated thermal design, evolved into the [RMWR](/wiki/RMWR "RMWR") concept, using light water in a low-density [supercritical](/wiki/Supercritical_fluid "Supercritical fluid") form to increase the neutron economy enough to allow breeding. 

Aside from water-cooled, there are many other types of breeder reactor currently envisioned as possible. These include [molten-salt cooled](/wiki/Molten-salt_reactor "Molten-salt reactor"), [gas cooled](/wiki/Gas-cooled_fast_reactor "Gas-cooled fast reactor"), and [liquid-metal cooled](/wiki/Liquid_metal_cooled_reactor "Liquid metal cooled reactor") designs in many variations. Almost any of these basic design types may be fueled by [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium"), [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium "Plutonium"), many minor [actinides](/wiki/Actinides "Actinides"), or [thorium](/wiki/Thorium "Thorium"), and they may be designed for many different goals, such as creating more fissile fuel, long-term steady-state operation, or active burning of [nuclear wastes](/wiki/Radioactive_waste "Radioactive waste"). 

Extant reactor designs are sometimes divided into two broad categories based upon their neutron spectrum, which generally separates those designed to use primarily uranium and [transuranics](/wiki/Transuranium_element "Transuranium element") from those designed to use thorium and avoid transuranics. These designs are: 

  * **Fast breeder reactors** (FBRs) which use ['fast' (i.e. unmoderated) neutrons](/wiki/Neutron_temperature "Neutron temperature") to breed fissile plutonium (and possibly higher transuranics) from fertile [uranium-238](/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238"). The fast spectrum is flexible enough that it can also breed fissile [uranium-233](/wiki/Uranium-233 "Uranium-233") from thorium, if desired.
  * **Thermal breeder reactors** which use 'thermal-spectrum' or 'slow' (i.e. [moderated](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator")) neutrons to breed fissile [uranium-233 from thorium](/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle "Thorium fuel cycle"). Due to the behavior of the various nuclear fuels, a thermal breeder is thought commercially feasible only with thorium fuel, which avoids the buildup of the heavier transuranics.

### Fast breeder reactor

[](/wiki/File:LMFBR_schematics2.svg)Schematic diagram showing the difference between the Loop and Pool types of LMFBR [](/wiki/File:ANLWFuelConditioningFacility.jpg)[Experimental Breeder Reactor II](/wiki/Experimental_Breeder_Reactor_II "Experimental Breeder Reactor II"), which served as the prototype for the Integral Fast Reactor

As of 2026 all large-scale FBR [power stations](/wiki/Nuclear_power_plant "Nuclear power plant") are [sodium](/wiki/Sodium "Sodium") cooled [liquid metal fast breeder reactors](/wiki/Liquid_metal_cooled_reactor "Liquid metal cooled reactor") (LMFBR) of one of the following two designs:[](#cite_note-Waltar-1): 43

  * _Loop_ type, in which the primary coolant is circulated through primary heat exchangers outside the reactor tank (but inside the [biological shield](/wiki/Biological_shield "Biological shield") due to radioactive [24Na](/wiki/Sodium-24 "Sodium-24") in the primary coolant)
  * _Pool_ type, in which the primary heat exchangers and pumps are immersed in the reactor tank

There are only two commercially operating breeder reactors as of 2017[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Breeder_reactor&action=edit): the [BN-600 reactor](/wiki/BN-600_reactor "BN-600 reactor"), at 560 MWe, and the [BN-800 reactor](/wiki/BN-800_reactor "BN-800 reactor"), at 880 MWe. Both are Russian sodium-cooled reactors. The designs use liquid metal as the primary coolant, to transfer heat from the core to steam used to power the electricity generating turbines. FBRs cooled by liquid metals other than sodium have been built, some early FBRs such as [Clementine](/wiki/Clementine_\(nuclear_reactor\) "Clementine \(nuclear reactor\)") used [mercury](/wiki/Mercury_\(element\) "Mercury \(element\)"), [Lead-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor "Lead-cooled fast reactor") have also been constructed, as well as other experimental reactors using [Lead-bismuth eutectic](/wiki/Lead-bismuth_eutectic "Lead-bismuth eutectic"), molten [tin](/wiki/Tin "Tin"), or NaK, a [sodium-potassium alloy](/wiki/Sodium-potassium_alloy "Sodium-potassium alloy"). Mercury and NaK have the advantage that they are liquids at room temperature, which is convenient for experimental rigs but less important for pilot or full-scale power stations, where their chemical toxicity and cost respectively leave them as less attractive options. [Lead-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor "Lead-cooled fast reactor") as well as those utilizing [Lead-bismuth eutectic](/wiki/Lead-bismuth_eutectic "Lead-bismuth eutectic") saw extensive use in the Soviet Union, with liquid [Lead](/wiki/Lead "Lead") seeing use in the [BREST (reactor)](/wiki/BREST_\(reactor\) "BREST \(reactor\)") and [Lead-bismuth eutectic](/wiki/Lead-bismuth_eutectic "Lead-bismuth eutectic") having a troubled career as a coolant in [Alfa-class submarine's](/wiki/Alfa-class_submarine "Alfa-class submarine") [OK-550](/wiki/OK-550_reactor "OK-550 reactor") and [BM-40A reactors](/wiki/BM-40A_reactor "BM-40A reactor")[](#cite_note-3) as well as the SVBR-100 reactor.[](#cite_note-4)

Three of the proposed [generation IV reactor](/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor "Generation IV reactor") types are FBRs:[](#cite_note-5)

  * [Gas-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Gas-cooled_fast_reactor "Gas-cooled fast reactor") cooled by [helium](/wiki/Helium "Helium").
  * [Sodium-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Sodium-cooled_fast_reactor "Sodium-cooled fast reactor") based on the existing LMFBR and [integral fast reactor](/wiki/Integral_fast_reactor "Integral fast reactor") designs.
  * [Lead-cooled fast reactor](/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor "Lead-cooled fast reactor") based on Soviet naval propulsion units.

FBRs usually use a [mixed oxide fuel](/wiki/Mixed_oxide_fuel "Mixed oxide fuel") core of up to 20% [plutonium dioxide](/wiki/Plutonium_dioxide "Plutonium dioxide") (PuO2) and at least 80% [uranium dioxide](/wiki/Uranium_dioxide "Uranium dioxide") (UO2). Another fuel option is [metal alloys](/wiki/Nuclear_fuel#Metal_fuel "Nuclear fuel"), typically a blend of uranium, plutonium, and [zirconium](/wiki/Zirconium "Zirconium") (used because it is "transparent" to neutrons). [Enriched uranium](/wiki/Enriched_uranium "Enriched uranium") can be used on its own. 

Many designs surround the [reactor core](/wiki/Reactor_core "Reactor core") in a blanket of tubes that contain non-fissile uranium-238, which, by capturing fast neutrons from the reaction in the core, converts to fissile [plutonium-239](/wiki/Plutonium-239 "Plutonium-239") (as is some of the uranium in the core), which is then reprocessed and used as nuclear fuel. Other FBR designs rely on the geometry of the fuel (which also contains uranium-238), arranged to attain sufficient fast neutron capture. The plutonium-239 (or the fissile uranium-235) [fissile cross-section](/wiki/Nuclear_cross_section "Nuclear cross section") is much smaller in a fast spectrum than in a thermal spectrum, as is the ratio between the 239Pu/235U fission cross-section and the 238U absorption cross-section. This increases the concentration of 239Pu/235U needed to sustain a [chain reaction](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction "Nuclear chain reaction"), as well as the ratio of breeding to fission.[](#cite_note-Hoffman-6) On the other hand, a fast reactor needs no moderator to [slow down the neutrons](/wiki/Thermalisation "Thermalisation") at all, taking advantage of the fast neutrons producing a greater number of neutrons per fission than slow neutrons. For this reason ordinary liquid [water](/wiki/Water "Water"), being a moderator and [neutron absorber](/wiki/Neutron_absorber "Neutron absorber"), is an undesirable primary coolant for fast reactors. Because large amounts of water in the core are required to cool the reactor, the yield of neutrons and therefore breeding of 239Pu are strongly affected. Theoretical work has been done on [reduced moderation water reactors](/wiki/Reduced_moderation_water_reactor "Reduced moderation water reactor"), which may have a sufficiently fast spectrum to provide a breeding ratio slightly over 1. This would likely result in an unacceptable power derating and high costs in a liquid-water-cooled reactor, but the supercritical water coolant of the [supercritical water reactor](/wiki/Supercritical_water_reactor "Supercritical water reactor") (SCWR) has sufficient heat capacity to allow adequate cooling with less water, making a fast-spectrum water-cooled reactor a practical possibility.[](#cite_note-Superfast-7)

The type of coolants, temperatures, and fast neutron spectrum puts the fuel cladding material (normally [austenitic stainless](/wiki/Austenitic_stainless_steel "Austenitic stainless steel") or ferritic-martensitic steels) under extreme conditions. The understanding of the radiation damage, coolant interactions, stresses, and temperatures are necessary for the safe operation of any reactor core. All materials used to date in sodium-cooled fast reactors have known limits.[](#cite_note-8) [Oxide dispersion-strengthened alloy](/wiki/Oxide_dispersion-strengthened_alloy "Oxide dispersion-strengthened alloy") steel is viewed as the long-term radiation resistant fuel-cladding material that can overcome the shortcomings of today's material choices. 

#### Integral fast reactor

One design of fast neutron reactor, specifically conceived to address the waste disposal and plutonium issues, was the [integral fast reactor](/wiki/Integral_fast_reactor "Integral fast reactor") (IFR, also known as an integral fast breeder reactor, although the original reactor was designed to not breed a net surplus of fissile material).[](#cite_note-ANL-9)[](#cite_note-10)

To solve the waste disposal problem, the IFR had an on-site [electrowinning](/wiki/Electrowinning "Electrowinning") fuel-reprocessing unit that recycled the uranium and all the transuranics (not just plutonium) via [electroplating](/wiki/Electroplating "Electroplating"), leaving just short-[half-life](/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") [fission products](/wiki/Fission_product "Fission product") in the waste. Some of these fission products could later be separated for industrial or medical uses and the rest sent to a waste repository. The IFR pyroprocessing system uses molten [cadmium](/wiki/Cadmium "Cadmium") cathodes and electrorefiners to reprocess metallic fuel directly on-site at the reactor.[](#cite_note-11) Such systems co-mingle all the minor actinides with both uranium and plutonium. The systems are compact and self-contained, so that no plutonium-containing material needs to be transported away from the site of the breeder reactor. Breeder reactors incorporating such technology would most likely be designed with breeding ratios very close to 1.00, so that after an initial loading of enriched uranium and/or plutonium fuel, the reactor would then be refueled only with small deliveries of [natural uranium](/wiki/Natural_uranium "Natural uranium"). A quantity of natural uranium equivalent to a block about the size of a milk crate delivered once per month would be all the fuel such a 1 gigawatt reactor would need.[](#cite_note-12) Such self-contained breeders are currently envisioned as the final self-contained and self-supporting ultimate goal of nuclear reactor designers.[](#cite_note-Argonne-13)[](#cite_note-Hoffman-6) The project was canceled in 1994 by [United States Secretary of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_Energy "United States Secretary of Energy") [Hazel O'Leary](/wiki/Hazel_O%27Leary "Hazel O'Leary").[](#cite_note-14)[](#cite_note-15)

#### Other fast reactors

[](/wiki/File:MSRE_Core.JPG)The graphite core of the [Molten Salt Reactor Experiment](/wiki/Molten_Salt_Reactor_Experiment "Molten Salt Reactor Experiment")

The first fast reactor built and operated was the Los Alamos Plutonium Fast Reactor ("[Clementine](/wiki/Clementine_\(nuclear_reactor\) "Clementine \(nuclear reactor\)")") in Los Alamos, NM.[](#cite_note-:1-16) Clementine was fueled by Ga-stabilized delta-phase Pu and cooled with mercury. It contained a 'window' of Th-232 in anticipation of breeding experiments, but no reports were made available regarding this feature. 

Another proposed fast reactor is a fast [molten salt reactor](/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor "Molten salt reactor"), in which the molten salt's moderating properties are insignificant. This is typically achieved by replacing the light metal fluorides (e.g. LiF, BeF2) in the salt carrier with heavier metal chlorides (e.g., KCl, RbCl, ZrCl4). 

Several prototype FBRs have been built, ranging in electrical output from a few light bulbs' equivalent ([EBR-I](/wiki/EBR-I "EBR-I"), 1951) to over 1,000 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe"). As of 2006, the technology is not economically competitive to thermal reactor technology, but [India](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_India "Nuclear power in India"), Japan, China, South Korea, and Russia are all committing substantial research funds to further development of fast breeder reactors, anticipating that rising uranium prices will change this in the long term. Germany, in contrast, abandoned the technology due to safety concerns. The [SNR-300](/wiki/SNR-300 "SNR-300") fast breeder reactor was finished after 19 years despite cost overruns summing up to a total of €3.6 billion, only to then be abandoned.[](#cite_note-17)

### Thermal breeder reactor

[](/wiki/File:Shippingport_Reactor.jpg)The Shippingport Reactor, used as a prototype light water breeder for five years beginning in August 1977

The [advanced heavy-water reactor](/wiki/Advanced_heavy-water_reactor "Advanced heavy-water reactor") is one of the few proposed large-scale uses of thorium.[](#cite_note-18) India is developing this technology, motivated by substantial thorium reserves; almost a third of the world's thorium reserves are in India, which lacks significant uranium reserves. 

The third and final core of the [Shippingport Atomic Power Station](/wiki/Shippingport_Atomic_Power_Station "Shippingport Atomic Power Station") 60 MWe reactor was a light water thorium breeder, which began operating in 1977.[](#cite_note-19) It used pellets made of [thorium dioxide](/wiki/Thorium_dioxide "Thorium dioxide") and uranium-233 oxide; initially, the U-233 content of the pellets was 5–6% in the seed region, 1.5–3% in the blanket region, and none in the reflector region. It operated at 236 MWt, generating 60 MWe, and ultimately produced over 2.1 billion kilowatt hours of electricity. After five years, the core was removed and found to contain nearly 1.4% more fissile material than when it was installed, demonstrating that breeding from thorium had occurred.[](#cite_note-20)[](#cite_note-21)

A [liquid fluoride thorium reactor](/wiki/Liquid_fluoride_thorium_reactor "Liquid fluoride thorium reactor") is also planned as a thorium thermal breeder. Liquid-fluoride reactors may have attractive features, such as inherent safety, no need to manufacture fuel rods, and possibly simpler reprocessing of the liquid fuel. This concept was first investigated at the [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Laboratory "Oak Ridge National Laboratory") [Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment](/wiki/Molten-Salt_Reactor_Experiment "Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment") in the 1960s. From 2012 it became the subject of renewed interest worldwide.[](#cite_note-22)

## Fuel resources

Breeder reactors could, in principle, extract almost all of the energy contained in uranium or thorium, decreasing fuel requirements by a factor of 100 compared to widely used once-through light water reactors, which extract less than 1% of the energy in the actinide metal (uranium or thorium) mined from the earth.[](#cite_note-Argonne-13) The high fuel-efficiency of breeder reactors could greatly reduce concerns about fuel supply, energy used in mining, and storage of radioactive waste. With [seawater uranium extraction](/wiki/Seawater_uranium_extraction "Seawater uranium extraction") (currently too expensive to be economical), there is enough fuel for breeder reactors to satisfy the world's energy needs for 5 billion years at 1983's total energy consumption rate, thus making nuclear energy effectively a [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy "Renewable energy").[](#cite_note-sustainablenuclear-23)[](#cite_note-24) In addition to seawater, the average crustal granite rocks contain significant quantities of uranium and thorium that with breeder reactors can supply abundant energy for the remaining lifespan of the sun on the main sequence of stellar evolution.[](#cite_note-25)

In broad terms, spent nuclear fuel has three main components. The first consists of [fission products](/wiki/Fission_products "Fission products"), the leftover fragments of fuel atoms after they have been split to release energy. Fission products come in dozens of elements and hundreds of isotopes, all of them lighter than uranium. The second main component of spent fuel is transuranics (atoms heavier than uranium), which are generated from uranium or heavier atoms in the fuel when they absorb neutrons but do not undergo fission. All transuranic isotopes fall within the actinide series on the [periodic table](/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table"), and so they are frequently referred to as the actinides. The largest component is the remaining uranium which is around 98.25% uranium-238, 1.1% uranium-235, and 0.65% uranium-236. The U-236 comes from the non-fission capture reaction where U-235 absorbs a neutron but releases only a high energy [gamma ray](/wiki/Gamma_ray "Gamma ray") instead of undergoing fission. 

The physical behavior of the fission products is markedly different from that of the actinides. In particular, fission products do not undergo fission and therefore cannot be used as nuclear fuel. Indeed, because fission products are often [neutron poisons](/wiki/Neutron_poison "Neutron poison") (absorbing neutrons that could be used to sustain a chain reaction), fission products are viewed as nuclear 'ashes' left over from consuming fissile materials. Furthermore, only seven [long-lived fission product](/wiki/Long-lived_fission_product "Long-lived fission product") isotopes have [half-lives](/wiki/Half-lives "Half-lives") longer than a hundred years, which makes their geological storage or disposal less problematic than for transuranic materials.[](#cite_note-31)

With increased concerns about nuclear waste, breeding fuel cycles came under renewed interest as they can reduce actinide wastes, particularly plutonium and minor actinides.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear-32) Breeder reactors are designed to fission the actinide wastes as fuel and thus convert them to more fission products. After [spent nuclear fuel](/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel "Spent nuclear fuel") is removed from a light water reactor, it undergoes a complex decay profile as each nuclide decays at a different rate. There is a large gap in the decay half-lives of fission products compared to transuranic isotopes. If the transuranics are left in the spent fuel, after 1,000 to 100,000 years the slow decay of these transuranics would generate most of the radioactivity in that spent fuel. Thus, removing the transuranics from the waste eliminates much of the long-term radioactivity of spent nuclear fuel.[](#cite_note-aps-33)

Today's commercial light-water reactors do breed some new fissile material, mostly in the form of plutonium. Because commercial reactors were never designed as breeders, they do not convert enough uranium-238 into plutonium to replace the uranium-235 consumed. Nonetheless, at least one-third of the power produced by commercial nuclear reactors comes from fission of plutonium generated within the fuel.[](#cite_note-34) Even with this level of plutonium consumption, light water reactors consume only part of the plutonium and minor actinides they produce, and nonfissile [isotopes of plutonium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_plutonium "Isotopes of plutonium") build up, along with significant quantities of other minor actinides.[](#cite_note-SCALE5-35)

Breeding fuel cycles attracted renewed interest because of their potential to reduce actinide wastes, particularly various isotopes of plutonium and the minor actinides (neptunium, americium, curium, etc.).[](#cite_note-world-nuclear-32) Since breeder reactors on a closed fuel cycle would use nearly all of the isotopes of these actinides fed into them as fuel, their fuel requirements would be reduced by a factor of about 100. The volume of waste they generate would be reduced by a factor of about 100 as well. While there is a huge reduction in the _volume_ of waste from a breeder reactor, the [_activity_](/wiki/Specific_activity "Specific activity") of the waste is about the same as that produced by a light-water reactor.[](#cite_note-36)

Waste from a breeder reactor has a different decay behavior because it is made up of different materials. Breeder reactor waste is mostly fission products, while light-water reactor waste is mostly unused uranium isotopes and a large quantity of transuranics. After spent nuclear fuel has been removed from a light-water reactor for longer than 100,000 years, the transuranics would be the main source of radioactivity. Eliminating them would eliminate much of the long-term radioactivity from the spent fuel.[](#cite_note-aps-33)

In principle, breeder fuel cycles can recycle and consume all actinides,[](#cite_note-sustainablenuclear-23) leaving only fission products. As the graphic in this section indicates, fission products have a peculiar "gap" in their aggregate half-lives, such that no fission products have a half-life between 91 and 200,000 years. As a result of this physical oddity, after several hundred years in storage, the activity of the [radioactive waste](/wiki/Radioactive_waste "Radioactive waste") from an FBR would quickly drop to the low level of the [long-lived fission products](/wiki/Long-lived_fission_products "Long-lived fission products"). However, to obtain this benefit requires the highly efficient separation of transuranics from spent fuel. If the [fuel reprocessing](/wiki/Fuel_reprocessing "Fuel reprocessing") methods used leave a large fraction of the transuranics in the final waste stream, this advantage would be greatly reduced.[](#cite_note-Argonne-13)

The FBR's fast neutrons can fission actinide nuclei with even numbers of both protons and neutrons. Such nuclei usually lack the low-speed "thermal neutron" [resonances](/wiki/Doppler_broadening "Doppler broadening") of fissile fuels used in LWRs.[](#cite_note-37) The thorium fuel cycle inherently produces lower levels of heavy actinides. The fertile material in the thorium fuel cycle has an atomic weight of 232, while the fertile material in the uranium fuel cycle has an atomic weight of 238. That mass difference means that thorium-232 requires six more neutron capture events per nucleus before the transuranic elements can be produced. In addition to this simple mass difference, the reactor gets two chances to fission the nuclei as the mass increases: First as the effective fuel nuclei U233, and as it absorbs two more neutrons, again as the fuel nuclei U235.[](#cite_note-38)[](#cite_note-39)

A reactor whose main purpose is to destroy actinides rather than increasing fissile fuel-stocks is sometimes known as a **burner reactor**. Both breeding and burning depend on good neutron economy, and many designs can do either. Breeding designs surround the core by a [breeding blanket](/wiki/Breeding_blanket "Breeding blanket") of fertile material. Waste burners surround the core with non-fertile wastes to be destroyed. Some designs add neutron reflectors or absorbers.[](#cite_note-Hoffman-6)

### Conversion ratio

One measure of a reactor's performance is the "conversion ratio", defined as the ratio of new fissile atoms produced to fissile atoms consumed. All proposed nuclear reactors except specially designed and operated actinide burners[](#cite_note-Hoffman-6) experience some degree of conversion. As long as there is any amount of a fertile material within the [neutron flux](/wiki/Neutron_flux "Neutron flux") of the reactor, some new fissile material is always created. When the conversion ratio is greater than 1, it is often called the "breeding ratio". 

For example, commonly used light water reactors have a conversion ratio of approximately 0.6. [Pressurized heavy-water reactors](/wiki/Pressurized_heavy-water_reactor "Pressurized heavy-water reactor") running on natural uranium have a conversion ratio of 0.8.[](#cite_note-42) In a breeder reactor, the conversion ratio is higher than 1. "Break-even" is achieved when the conversion ratio reaches 1.0 and the reactor produces as much fissile material as it uses. 

### Doubling time

The [doubling time](/wiki/Doubling_time "Doubling time") is the amount of time it would take for a breeder reactor to produce enough new fissile material to replace the original fuel and additionally produce an equivalent amount of fuel for another nuclear reactor. This was considered an important measure of breeder performance in early years, when uranium was thought to be scarce. However, since uranium is more abundant than thought in the early days of nuclear reactor development, and given the amount of plutonium available in spent reactor fuel, doubling time has become a less important metric in modern breeder-reactor design.[](#cite_note-43)[](#cite_note-44)

### Burnup

"[Burnup](/wiki/Burnup "Burnup")" is a measure of how much energy has been extracted from a given mass of heavy metal in fuel, often expressed (for power reactors) in terms of gigawatt-days per ton of heavy metal. Burnup is an important factor in determining the types and abundances of isotopes produced by a fission reactor. Breeder reactors by design have high burnup compared to a conventional reactor, as breeder reactors produce more of their waste in the form of fission products, while most or all of the actinides are meant to be fissioned and destroyed.[](#cite_note-45)

In the past, breeder-reactor development focused on reactors with low breeding ratios, from 1.01 for the [Shippingport Reactor](/wiki/Shippingport_Reactor "Shippingport Reactor")[](#cite_note-46)[](#cite_note-47) running on thorium fuel and cooled by conventional light water to over 1.2 for the Soviet [BN-350](/wiki/BN-350 "BN-350") liquid-metal-cooled reactor.[](#cite_note-48) Theoretical models of breeders with liquid sodium coolant flowing through tubes inside fuel elements ("tube-in-shell" construction) suggest breeding ratios of at least 1.8 are possible on an industrial scale.[](#cite_note-49) The Soviet BR-1 test reactor achieved a breeding ratio of 2.5 under non-commercial conditions.[](#cite_note-Korobeinikov_2014-50)

### Reprocessing

Fission of the nuclear fuel in any reactor unavoidably produces neutron-absorbing [fission products](/wiki/Fission_products "Fission products"). The fertile material from a breeder reactor then needs to be [reprocessed](/wiki/Nuclear_reprocessing "Nuclear reprocessing") to remove those [neutron poisons](/wiki/Neutron_poison "Neutron poison"). This step is required to fully utilize the ability to breed as much or more fuel than is consumed. All reprocessing can present a [proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") concern, since it can extract weapons-usable material from spent fuel.[](#cite_note-Bari-51) The most common reprocessing technique, [PUREX](/wiki/PUREX "PUREX"), presents a particular concern since it was expressly designed to separate plutonium. Early proposals for the breeder-reactor fuel cycle posed an even greater proliferation concern because they would use PUREX to separate plutonium in a highly attractive isotopic form for use in nuclear weapons.[](#cite_note-Bathke1-52)[](#cite_note-53)

Several countries are developing reprocessing methods that do not separate the plutonium from the other actinides. For instance, the non-water-based [pyrometallurgical](/wiki/Pyrometallurgical "Pyrometallurgical") electrowinning process, when used to reprocess fuel from an [integral fast reactor](/wiki/Integral_fast_reactor "Integral fast reactor"), leaves large amounts of radioactive actinides in the reactor fuel.[](#cite_note-Argonne-13) More conventional water-based reprocessing systems include SANEX, UNEX, DIAMEX, COEX, and TRUEX, and proposals to combine PUREX with those and other co-processes. All these systems have moderately better proliferation resistance than PUREX, though their adoption rate is low.[](#cite_note-54)[](#cite_note-55)[](#cite_note-56)

In the thorium cycle, thorium-232 breeds by converting first to protactinium-233, which then decays to uranium-233. If the protactinium remains in the reactor, small amounts of uranium-232 are also produced, which has the strong gamma emitter [thallium-208](/wiki/Isotopes_of_thallium "Isotopes of thallium") in its decay chain. Similar to uranium-fueled designs, the longer the fuel and fertile material remain in the reactor, the more of these undesirable elements build up. In the envisioned commercial [thorium reactors](/wiki/Thorium_reactors "Thorium reactors"), high levels of uranium-232 would be allowed to accumulate, leading to extremely high gamma-radiation doses from any uranium derived from thorium. These gamma rays complicate the safe handling of a weapon and the design of its electronics; this explains why uranium-233 has never been pursued for weapons beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations.[](#cite_note-57)

While the thorium cycle may be proliferation-resistant with regard to uranium-233 extraction from fuel (because of the presence of uranium-232), it poses a proliferation risk from an alternate route of uranium-233 extraction, which involves chemically extracting protactinium-233 and allowing it to decay to pure uranium-233 outside of the reactor. This process is an obvious chemical operation which is not required for normal operation of these reactor designs, but it could feasibly happen beyond the oversight of organizations such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), and thus must be safeguarded against.[](#cite_note-58)

## Production

Like many aspects of nuclear power, fast breeder reactors have been subject to much controversy over the years. In 2010 the [International Panel on Fissile Materials](/wiki/International_Panel_on_Fissile_Materials "International Panel on Fissile Materials") said "After six decades and the expenditure of the equivalent of tens of billions of dollars, the promise of breeder reactors remains largely unfulfilled and efforts to commercialize them have been steadily cut back in most countries". In Germany, the United Kingdom, and the United States, breeder reactor development programs have been abandoned.[](#cite_note-59)[](#cite_note-fbrb2010-60) The rationale for pursuing breeder reactors—sometimes explicit and sometimes implicit—was based on the following key assumptions:[](#cite_note-fbrb2010-60)[](#cite_note-61)

  * It was expected that uranium would be scarce and high-grade deposits would quickly become depleted if fission power were deployed on a large scale; the reality, however, is that since the end of the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), uranium has been much cheaper and more abundant than early designers expected.[](#cite_note-62)
  * It was expected that breeder reactors would quickly become economically competitive with the light-water reactors that dominate nuclear power today, but the reality is that capital costs are at least 25% more than water-cooled reactors.
  * It was thought that breeder reactors could be as safe and reliable as light-water reactors, but safety issues are cited as a concern with fast reactors that use a sodium coolant, where a leak could lead to a sodium fire.
  * It was expected that the proliferation risks posed by breeders and their "closed" fuel cycle, in which plutonium would be recycled, could be managed. But since plutonium-breeding reactors produce plutonium from U238, and thorium reactors produce fissile U233 from thorium, all breeding cycles could theoretically pose proliferation risks.[](#cite_note-Council-63) However U-232, which is always present in U-233 produced in breeder reactors, is a strong gamma-emitter via its daughter products, and would make weapon handling extremely hazardous and the weapon easy to detect.[](#cite_note-64)

Some past anti-nuclear advocates have become pro-nuclear power as a clean source of electricity since breeder reactors effectively recycle most of their waste. This solves one of the most-important negative issues of nuclear power. In the documentary _[Pandora's Promise](/wiki/Pandora%27s_Promise "Pandora's Promise")_ , a case is made for breeder reactors because they provide a real high-kW alternative to fossil fuel energy. According to the movie, one pound of uranium provides as much energy as 5,000 [barrels of oil](/wiki/Barrel_of_oil_equivalent "Barrel of oil equivalent").[](#cite_note-65)

## Future plants

### India

India has been developing fast breeder reactors as part of its [three-stage nuclear power programme](/wiki/India%27s_three-stage_nuclear_power_programme "India's three-stage nuclear power programme"). The 500 MWe [Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR)](/wiki/Prototype_Fast_Breeder_Reactor "Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor") at [Kalpakkam](/wiki/Kalpakkam "Kalpakkam") achieved first criticality on 6 April 2026,[](#cite_note-73)[](#cite_note-74)[](#cite_note-:0-75) marking a major milestone in India’s fast breeder programme. The programme aims to use fertile thorium-232 to breed fissile uranium-233. India’s focus on thorium is due to its large domestic thorium reserves, although thorium resources are also widely available globally. 

[BHAVINI](/wiki/BHAVINI "BHAVINI"), an Indian nuclear power company, was established in 2003 to construct, commission, and operate all stage II fast breeder reactors outlined in [India's three-stage nuclear power programme](/wiki/India%27s_three-stage_nuclear_power_programme "India's three-stage nuclear power programme"). To advance these plans, the [FBR-600](/wiki/FBR-600 "FBR-600") is a pool-type sodium-cooled reactor with a rating of 600 MWe. 

[](/wiki/File:CEFR_\(04790005\).jpg)The Chinese Experimental Fast Reactor is a 65 [MW](/wiki/Watt#Multiples "Watt") (thermal), 20 MW (electric), sodium-cooled, pool-type reactor with a 30-year design lifetime and a target [burnup](/wiki/Burnup "Burnup") of 100 MWd/kg.

### China

The [China Experimental Fast Reactor](/wiki/China_Experimental_Fast_Reactor "China Experimental Fast Reactor") is a 25 MW(e) prototype for the planned China Prototype Fast Reactor.[](#cite_note-76) It started generating power in 2011.[](#cite_note-77) China initiated a research and development project in thorium molten-salt thermal breeder-reactor technology (liquid fluoride thorium reactor), formally announced at the [Chinese Academy of Sciences](/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences "Chinese Academy of Sciences") annual conference in 2011. Its ultimate target was to investigate and develop a thorium-based molten salt nuclear system over about 20 years.[](#cite_note-78)[](#cite_note-79)[_[needs update](/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_]

### South Korea

South Korea is developing a design for a standardized modular FBR for export, to complement the standardized pressurized water reactor and [CANDU](/wiki/CANDU "CANDU") designs they have already developed and built, but has not yet committed to building a prototype.

### Russia

Russia has a plan for increasing its fleet of fast breeder reactors significantly. A BN-800 reactor (800 MWe) at [Beloyarsk](/wiki/Beloyarsk_Nuclear_Power_Station "Beloyarsk Nuclear Power Station") was completed in 2012, succeeding a smaller [BN-600](/wiki/BN-600 "BN-600").[](#cite_note-80) It reached its full power production in 2016.[](#cite_note-fullpower-81) Plans for the construction of a larger [BN-1200 reactor](/wiki/BN-1200_reactor "BN-1200 reactor") (1,200 MWe) was scheduled for completion in 2018, with two additional BN-1200 reactors built by the end of 2030.[](#cite_note-82) However, in 2015 [Rosenergoatom](/wiki/Rosenergoatom "Rosenergoatom") postponed construction indefinitely to allow fuel design to be improved after more experience of operating the BN-800 reactor, and among cost concerns.[](#cite_note-wnn-20150416-83)

An experimental lead-cooled fast reactor, [BREST-300](/wiki/BREST_\(Reactor\) "BREST \(Reactor\)") will be built at the [Siberian Chemical Combine](/wiki/Siberian_Chemical_Combine "Siberian Chemical Combine") in [Seversk](/wiki/Seversk "Seversk"). The BREST ([Russian](/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): _bystry reaktor so svintsovym teplonositelem_ , English: fast reactor with lead coolant) design is seen as a successor to the BN series and the 300 MWe unit at the SCC could be the forerunner to a 1,200 MWe version for wide deployment as a commercial power generation unit. The development program is as part of an Advanced Nuclear Technologies Federal Program 2010–2020 that seeks to exploit fast reactors for uranium efficiency while 'burning' radioactive substances that would otherwise be disposed of as waste. Its core would measure about 2.3 metres in diameter by 1.1 metres in height and contain 16 tonnes of fuel. The unit would be refuelled every year, with each fuel element spending five years in total within the core. Lead coolant temperature would be around 540 °C, giving a high efficiency of 43%, primary heat production of 700 MWt yielding electrical power of 300 MWe. The operational lifespan of the unit could be 60 years. The design was expected to be completed by NIKIET in 2014 for construction between 2016 and 2020.[](#cite_note-84) By the end of 2024 the cooling tower had been built, and the target for starting operation was 2026.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

### France

### United States

[](/wiki/File:Ebr1core.png)Assembly of the core of [Experimental Breeder Reactor I](/wiki/Experimental_Breeder_Reactor_I "Experimental Breeder Reactor I") in [Idaho](/wiki/Idaho "Idaho"), United States, 1951

Kirk Sorensen, former NASA scientist and chief nuclear technologist at [Teledyne Brown Engineering](/wiki/Teledyne_Brown_Engineering "Teledyne Brown Engineering"), has long been a promoter of thorium fuel cycle and particularly liquid fluoride thorium reactors. In 2011, Sorensen founded Flibe Energy, a company aimed to develop 20–50 MW LFTR reactor designs to power military bases.[](#cite_note-90)[](#cite_note-91)[](#cite_note-92)

In October 2010 [GE Hitachi Nuclear Energy](/wiki/GE_Hitachi_Nuclear_Energy "GE Hitachi Nuclear Energy") signed a [memorandum of understanding](/wiki/Memorandum_of_understanding "Memorandum of understanding") with the operators of the [US Department of Energy's](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy") [Savannah River Site](/wiki/Savannah_River_Site "Savannah River Site"), which should allow the construction of a demonstration plant based on the company's [S-PRISM](/wiki/S-PRISM "S-PRISM") fast breeder reactor prior to the design receiving full [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") licensing approval.[](#cite_note-wnn281010-93) In October 2011 _[The Independent](/wiki/The_Independent "The Independent")_ reported that the UK Nuclear Decommissioning Authority (NDA) and senior advisers within the Department for Energy and Climate Change (DECC) had asked for technical and financial details of PRISM, partly as a means of reducing the country's plutonium stockpile.[](#cite_note-94)

The [traveling wave reactor](/wiki/Traveling_wave_reactor "Traveling wave reactor") proposed in a patent by [Intellectual Ventures](/wiki/Intellectual_Ventures "Intellectual Ventures") is a fast breeder reactor designed to not need fuel reprocessing during the decades-long lifetime of the reactor. The breed-burn wave in the TWR design does not move from one end of the reactor to the other but gradually from the inside out. Moreover, as the fuel's composition changes through nuclear transmutation, fuel rods are continually reshuffled within the core to optimize the neutron flux and fuel usage at any given point in time. Thus, instead of letting the wave propagate through the fuel, the fuel itself is moved through a largely stationary burn wave. This is contrary to many media reports, which have popularized the concept as a candle-like reactor with a burn region that moves down a stick of fuel. By replacing a static core configuration with an actively managed "standing wave" or "soliton" core, [TerraPower](/wiki/TerraPower "TerraPower")'s design avoids the problem of cooling a highly variable burn region. Under this scenario, the reconfiguration of fuel rods is accomplished remotely by robotic devices; the containment vessel remains closed during the procedure, and there is no associated downtime.[](#cite_note-95)
