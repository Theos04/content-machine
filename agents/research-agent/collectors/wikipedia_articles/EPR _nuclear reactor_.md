Third-generation pressurised water nuclear reactor design

[](/wiki/File:EPR_OLK3_lage.jpg)Computer generated view of an EPR power station [](/wiki/File:EPR_pressure_vessel.png)Reactor pressure vessel of the EPR

The **EPR** is a [Generation III+](/wiki/Generation_III_reactor "Generation III reactor") [pressurised water reactor](/wiki/Pressurised_water_reactor "Pressurised water reactor") design. It has been designed and developed mainly by [Framatome](/wiki/Framatome "Framatome") (part of [Areva](/wiki/Areva "Areva") between 2001 and 2017) and [Électricité de France](/wiki/%C3%89lectricit%C3%A9_de_France "Électricité de France") (EDF) in France, and by [Siemens](/wiki/Siemens "Siemens") in Germany.[](#cite_note-1) In Europe, this reactor design was called **European Pressurised Reactor** , and the internationalised name was **Evolutionary Power Reactor** , but it has been simplified to **EPR**. 

The first operational EPR unit was China's [Taishan 1](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant"), which started commercial operation in December 2018.[](#cite_note-wnn-20181214-2) Taishan 2 started commercial operation in September 2019.[](#cite_note-wnn-20190909-3) European units have been so far plagued with prolonged construction delays and substantial cost overruns. The first EPR unit to start construction, at [Olkiluoto](/wiki/Olkiluoto_Nuclear_Power_Plant#Unit_3 "Olkiluoto Nuclear Power Plant") in Finland, originally intended to be commissioned in 2009, started commercial operation in 2023, a delay of fourteen years.[](#cite_note-OL3-regularproduction-4) The second EPR unit to start construction, at [Flamanville](/wiki/Flamanville_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant") in France, also suffered a more than decade-long delay in its commissioning (from 2012 to 2024).[](#cite_note-5) Two units at [Hinkley Point](/wiki/Hinkley_Point_C_nuclear_power_station "Hinkley Point C nuclear power station") in the United Kingdom received final approval in September 2016; the first unit was expected to begin operating in 2027,[](#cite_note-guardian-20160915-6)[](#cite_note-bbc-20220520-7) but was subsequently delayed to around 2030.[](#cite_note-guardian-20240123-8)

EDF has acknowledged severe difficulties in building the EPR design. In September 2015, EDF stated that the design of a "New Model" EPR (later named **EPR2**) was being worked on and that it would be easier and cheaper to build.[](#cite_note-independent-20150923-9)

EPR type reactor has a design service lifetime of 60 years.[](#cite_note-10)[](#cite_note-11)[](#cite_note-12)

## Design

### First EPR design

The main objectives of the [third generation](/wiki/Generation_III_reactor "Generation III reactor") EPR design are increased safety while providing enhanced economic competitiveness through improvements to previous [pressurised water reactor](/wiki/Pressurised_water_reactor "Pressurised water reactor") designs scaled up to an electrical power output of around 1650 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe") (net)[](#cite_note-areva-fieldrep-20101006-13) with thermal power of 4500 MW. The reactor can use [5% enriched uranium oxide](/wiki/Enriched_uranium "Enriched uranium") fuel, [reprocessed uranium](/wiki/Reprocessed_uranium "Reprocessed uranium") fuel or 100% [mixed uranium plutonium oxide](/wiki/MOX_fuel "MOX fuel") fuel, clad in Areva's M5 variant of [zirconium alloy](/wiki/Zirconium_alloy "Zirconium alloy").[](#cite_note-onr-2009-14)[](#cite_note-nei-20110509-15) The EPR is the evolutionary descendant of the [Framatome](/wiki/Framatome "Framatome") N4 and [Siemens Power Generation Division](/wiki/Siemens_AG "Siemens AG") "[Konvoi](/wiki/Konvoi "Konvoi")" reactors.[](#cite_note-EPR-presentation-16)[](#cite_note-EPR-brochure-17) Siemens ceased its nuclear activities in 2011.[](#cite_note-DeutscheWelle1-18) The EPR was designed to use [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium") more efficiently than older [Generation II reactors](/wiki/Generation_II_reactor "Generation II reactor"), using approximately 17% less uranium per [kilowatt-hour](/wiki/Kilowatt-hour "Kilowatt-hour") of electricity generated than these older reactor technologies.[](#cite_note-19)

The design has gone through a number of iterations. The 1994 conceptual design had an electrical power output of 1450 MW, the same as the Framatome N4, but using Siemens Konvoi derived instrumentation and also including a new [core catcher](/wiki/Core_catcher "Core catcher") safety system. By 1995, there was concern over excessive cost per MW, and output was raised to 1800 MW in the 1997 design, though this was subsequently reduced to 1650 MW (net) in the final certified design.[](#cite_note-thomas-2010-20) It has 4 coolant loops with 1 steam generator per loop. There are concrete walls between loops and the hot and cold parts of each loop to protect against failures. Besides the double layer containment there is a concrete wall surrounding the primary system components inside the containment.[](#cite_note-aris.iaea.org-21)

The EPR design has several active and passive protection measures against accidents: 

  * Four independent emergency cooling systems, each providing the required cooling of the [decay heat](/wiki/Decay_heat "Decay heat") (i.e., 300% redundancy)[](#cite_note-ShirzadiJackson2014-22)
  * Leak-tight containment around the reactor
  * An extra container and cooling area if a molten core manages to escape the reactor (see [containment building](/wiki/Containment_building "Containment building") and [core catcher](/wiki/Core_catcher "Core catcher"))
  * Two-layer concrete wall with a total thickness of 2.6 m (8 ft 6 in), designed to withstand impact by aeroplanes and internal overpressure, and a low vacuum in the annulus space between the two layers[](#cite_note-23)

The EPR has a design maximum [core damage frequency](/wiki/Core_damage_frequency "Core damage frequency") of 5.3 × 10−7 per station per year[](#cite_note-24) and a gross power output of 1770 MWe for a mains frequency of 50 Hz.[](#cite_note-aris.iaea.org-21) The version submitted to the U.S. NRC has an electrical power output of 1600 MW (net).[](#cite_note-25)

### EPR2 design

[](/wiki/File:EPR_1_EPR_2_perso.png)A floor plan of the EPR2 reactor building (right). It has a single-wall prestressed concrete containment structure with a metal liner and fewer angled walls, compared to the original EPR design (left) which has a double-wall prestressed containment structure with a metal liner.[](#cite_note-debatpublic.fr-30)

In 2013[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=EPR_\(nuclear_reactor\)&action=edit), EDF acknowledged the difficulties it was having building the EPR design, with its head of production and engineering, Hervé Machenaud, saying EDF had lost its dominant international position in design and construction of nuclear power stations. Machenaud indicated EDF was considering designing two new lower powered reactors, one with output of 1500 MW and the other 1000 MW. Machenaud stated there would be a period of reflection on the best way to improve the EPR design to lower its price and incorporate [post-Fukushima](/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster "Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster") safety improvements.[](#cite_note-reuters-20130321-31)

In September 2015, EDF's chief executive [Jean-Bernard Lévy](/wiki/Jean-Bernard_L%C3%A9vy "Jean-Bernard Lévy") stated that the design of a "New Model" EPR, or "EPR2",[](#cite_note-edf-epr-32) was being worked on, which would be easier to build, and be ready for orders from about 2020,[](#cite_note-independent-20150923-9) describing it in 2016 as "a reactor offering the same characteristics as today's EPR but it will be cheaper to build with optimised construction times and costs".[](#cite_note-theenergycollective-20161018-33)

In 2016, EDF planned to build two new model EPR reactors in France by 2030 to prepare for renewing its fleet of older reactors.[](#cite_note-reuters-20160121-34) However, following financial difficulties at Areva and its merger with EDF, French Ecology Minister [Nicolas Hulot](/wiki/Nicolas_Hulot "Nicolas Hulot") said in January 2018, "for now [building a new model EPR] is neither a priority or a plan. Right now the priority is to develop renewable energy and to reduce the share of nuclear."[](#cite_note-reuters-20180122-35) The industry-government plan for 2019–2022 included work on "a new version of the EPR".[](#cite_note-wnn-20190128-36)

In July 2019, the French nuclear safety authority [ASN](/wiki/Autorit%C3%A9_de_s%C3%BBret%C3%A9_nucl%C3%A9aire "Autorité de sûreté nucléaire") issued an opinion on the safety of an outlined new EPR model (EPR2) design. It found that general safety was on the whole satisfactory, though identifying areas for further examination. The most notable simplification is a single layer containment building with a liner as opposed to the EPR's double layer with a liner. ASN highlighted that the EPR design basis assumption that primary and secondary cooling circuit piping would not fail may no longer be appropriate for the simplified EPR2, and requires additional safety demonstrations.[](#cite_note-reuters-20190718-37)[](#cite_note-asn-20190729-38) Another simplification is that, unlike the first EPR design, the EPR2 design does not allow access to the reactor building for maintenance during reactor operation, which simplifies the design of the reactor building.[](#cite_note-39)[](#cite_note-atw-2024-40)

In 2020, French Energy Minister [Élisabeth Borne](/wiki/%C3%89lisabeth_Borne "Élisabeth Borne") announced the French government would not decide on the construction of any new reactors until the much delayed [Flamanville 3](/wiki/Flamanville_3 "Flamanville 3") started operation after 2022. EDF had estimated that building six EPR2 nuclear reactors would cost at least €46 billion.[](#cite_note-nei-20200114-41) A [Court of Audit](/wiki/Court_of_Audit_\(France\) "Court of Audit \(France\)") report concluded that EDF is no longer able to finance EPR2 construction on its own, so financing and profitability issues need to be resolved.[](#cite_note-wnn-20200715-42) The audit office requires that EDF ensure the financing and profitability of EPR2 before constructing any in France.[](#cite_note-wnn-20201016-43)

In January 2022, junior environment minister [Bérangère Abba](/wiki/B%C3%A9rang%C3%A8re_Abba "Bérangère Abba") said that plans for new EPR2 reactors, to be operational between 2035 and 2037, should be submitted around 2023.[](#cite_note-reuters-20220106-44) The decision was accelerated by the impact of the [2021 global energy crisis](/wiki/2021_global_energy_crisis "2021 global energy crisis").[](#cite_note-reuters-20211109-45) In June 2023, EDF announced it was starting the authorisation process to build two EPR2 reactors at [Penly Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Penly_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Penly Nuclear Power Plant").[](#cite_note-wnn-20230630-46)[](#cite_note-edf-20230314-47)

The EPR2 requires 250 types of pipes instead of 400 for the EPR, 571 valves instead of 13,300 valves for the EPR, and 100 types of doors instead of 300 in the EPR. The EPR2 also uses more prefabricated components, and the electrical buildings can be completely prefabricated. The fourth emergency/safety cooling system/train of the reactor is removed which means maintenance can only be performed when the plant is shut down. This train was added at the request of German electricians in the original EPR design to allow for on-power maintenance. The core catcher has been modified.[](#cite_note-debatpublic.fr-30)[](#cite_note-48)[](#cite_note-49)[](#cite_note-50) It has a net power output of 1670 MWe.[](#cite_note-51)

### EPR1200 design

A smaller variant of the EPR2 is being developed using three instead of four coolant loops generating 1200 MW net of electrical power, the EPR1200, intended for export. In February 2023, regulator ASN issued a positive opinion on the safety features of the EPR1200.[](#cite_note-wnn-20230202-52)

## Operational plants

### Olkiluoto 3 (Finland)

See also: [Nuclear power in Finland](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Finland "Nuclear power in Finland") and [Olkiluoto Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Olkiluoto_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Olkiluoto Nuclear Power Plant")

[](/wiki/File:OL3.jpg)Olkiluoto 3 under construction in 2009. It achieved first [criticality](/wiki/Criticality_\(status\) "Criticality \(status\)") in December 2021[](#cite_note-wnn-20211221-58) and started regular electricity production in April 2023.[](#cite_note-OL3-regularproduction-4)

Construction of the [Olkiluoto](/wiki/Olkiluoto_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Olkiluoto Nuclear Power Plant") 3[](#cite_note-59) power station in Finland began in August 2005.[](#cite_note-OL3-regularproduction-4) The station has an electrical power output of 1600 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe") (net).[](#cite_note-areva-fieldrep-20101006-13) The construction was a joint effort of French [Areva](/wiki/Areva "Areva") and German [Siemens AG](/wiki/Siemens_AG "Siemens AG") through their common subsidiary Areva NP, for Finnish operator [TVO](/wiki/Teollisuuden_Voima "Teollisuuden Voima"). Siemens ceased nuclear activities in 2011. Initial cost estimates were about €3.7 billion,[](#cite_note-60) but the project has since seen several severe cost increases and delays, with latest published cost estimates (from 2012) of more than €8 billion.[](#cite_note-61) The station was initially scheduled to go online in 2009.[](#cite_note-62)

In May 2006, construction delays of about one year were announced, following quality control problems across the construction. In part, the delays were due to the lack of oversight of subcontractors inexperienced in nuclear construction.[](#cite_note-63)[](#cite_note-64) The delays led to disappointing financial results for Areva. It blamed delays on the Finnish approach to approving technical documentation and designs.[](#cite_note-65)[](#cite_note-66)

In December 2006, TVO announced construction was about 18 months behind schedule so completion was now expected 2010–11, and there were reports that Areva was preparing to take a €500 million charge on its accounts for the delay.[](#cite_note-67)[](#cite_note-68)

At the end of June 2007, it was reported that [Säteilyturvakeskus](/wiki/Radiation_and_Nuclear_Safety_Authority "Radiation and Nuclear Safety Authority") (STUK), the Finnish Radiation and Nuclear Safety Authority, had found a number of safety-related design and manufacturing 'deficiencies'.[](#cite_note-69) In August 2007, a further construction delay of up to a year was reported associated with construction problems in reinforcing the reactor building to withstand an aeroplane crash, and the timely supply of adequate documentation to the Finnish authorities.[](#cite_note-70)[](#cite_note-71)[](#cite_note-72)

In September 2007, TVO reported the construction delay as "at least two years" and costs more than 25% over budget.[](#cite_note-73) Cost estimates by analysts for the overrun range up to €1.5 billion.[](#cite_note-74)[](#cite_note-75)

A further delay was announced in October 2008, making the total delay three years, giving an expected online date of 2012.[](#cite_note-76) The parties entered into arbitration to resolve a dispute over responsibility for the delays and final cost overruns.[](#cite_note-77)[](#cite_note-78) Areva settled the long-running dispute in 2018 by agreeing to pay €450 million for cost overruns and delays.[](#cite_note-79)

As of May 2009, the station was at least three and a half years behind schedule and more than 50 percent over-budget. [Areva](/wiki/Areva "Areva") and the utility involved "are in bitter dispute over who will bear the cost overruns and there is a real risk now that the utility will default".[](#cite_note-tom-80) In August 2009, Areva announced €550 million additional provisions for the build, taking station costs to €5.3 billion, and wiped out interim operating profits for the first half-year of 2009.[](#cite_note-81)

The dome of the containment structure was topped out in September 2009.[](#cite_note-82) 90% of procurement, 80% of engineering works and 73% of civil works were completed.[](#cite_note-83)

In June 2010, Areva announced €400 million of further provisions, taking the cost overrun to €2.7 billion. The timescale slipped from June 2012 to the end of 2012.[](#cite_note-84)[](#cite_note-85)[](#cite_note-wnn-20100608-86) In December 2011, TVO announced a further delay to August 2014.[](#cite_note-reuters-20111221-87) As of July 2012, the station was scheduled to start electricity production no earlier than 2015, a schedule slippage of at least six years.[](#cite_note-world-nuclear-news20120716-88) In December 2012 Areva's Chief Executive estimated costs to €8 billion.[](#cite_note-89)

In September 2014, Areva announced that operations would start in 2018.[](#cite_note-90) In October 2017, the date was pushed back to the spring of 2019.[](#cite_note-91) During testing between 2018 and 2021, multiple further delays were announced, of around three years in total.[](#cite_note-92)[](#cite_note-93)[](#cite_note-94)[](#cite_note-95)[](#cite_note-96)[](#cite_note-97)

Olkiluoto 3 achieved first [criticality](/wiki/Criticality_\(status\) "Criticality \(status\)") in December 2021.[](#cite_note-wnn-20211221-58) Grid connection took place in March 2022.[](#cite_note-productionStart-98) In May 2022, foreign material was found in the turbine steam reheater, and the plant was shut down for about three months of repair work. Regular production had been expected to begin in December 2022, after a test production phase.[](#cite_note-99) On 28 October 2022, it was announced cracks of a few centimetres had been found in all four of the feedwater pump impellers. The cause of the cracks was yet to be determined, and it was unclear how the commissioning schedule would be affected.[](#cite_note-wnn-20221028-100) The feedwater pumps are larger than in other nuclear reactors.[](#cite_note-nei-20221101-101)

Olkiluoto 3 started regular electricity production in April 2023.[](#cite_note-OL3-regularproduction-4)

### Flamanville 3 (France)

See also: [Nuclear power in France](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France "Nuclear power in France") and [Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Flamanville_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant")

[](/wiki/File:Flamanville-3_2010-07-15.jpg)Flamanville 3 under construction in 2010. It achieved first [criticality](/wiki/Criticality_\(status\) "Criticality \(status\)") in September 2024 and was connected to the grid in December 2024.[](#cite_note-Flamanville-gridconnection-102)

First concrete was poured for the demonstration EPR reactor at the [Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Flamanville_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Flamanville Nuclear Power Plant") on 6 December 2007.[](#cite_note-103) As the name implies, this will be the third nuclear reactor on the Flamanville site, and the second instance of an EPR being built. Electrical output will be 1630 MWe (net).[](#cite_note-areva-fieldrep-20101006-13) The project was planned to involve around €3.3 billion of capital expenditure from [EDF](/wiki/%C3%89lectricit%C3%A9_de_France "Électricité de France").[](#cite_note-EDF-F3-project-104)

From 19 October 2005 to 18 February 2006, the project was submitted to a national public debate. On 4 May 2006, the decision was made by EDF's Board of Directors to continue with the construction. Between 15 June and 31 July 2006, the unit underwent a public enquiry, which rendered a "favourable opinion" on the project.[](#cite_note-105) That summer, site preparation works began. 

In December 2007, construction of the unit itself began. This was expected to last 54 months, with commissioning planned for 2012.[](#cite_note-EDF-F3-project-104)

In April 2008, the French nuclear safety authority (_[Autorité de sûreté nucléaire](/wiki/Autorit%C3%A9_de_s%C3%BBret%C3%A9_nucl%C3%A9aire "Autorité de sûreté nucléaire")_ , ASN) reported that a quarter of the welds inspected in the secondary containment steel liner are not in accordance with norms, and that cracks have been found in the concrete base. EDF stated that progress was being made on these issues, which were raised very early in construction;[](#cite_note-106) however, on 21 May, ASN ordered a suspension of concrete pouring on the site.[](#cite_note-107) A month later, concreting work resumed after ASN accepted EDF's corrective action plan, which included external oversight checks.[](#cite_note-108)

In May 2009, [Stephen Thomas](/wiki/Stephen_Thomas_\(economist\) "Stephen Thomas \(economist\)") reported that after 18 months of construction, and after a series of quality control problems, the project is "more than 20 percent over budget and EDF is struggling to keep it on schedule".[](#cite_note-tom-80)

In August 2010, the regulator, ASN, reported further welding problems on the secondary containment steel liner.[](#cite_note-bloomberg-20100830-109) The same month, EDF announced that costs had increased 50% to €5 billion, and commissioning was delayed by about two years to 2014.[](#cite_note-bloomberg-20100830-109)

In July 2011, EDF announced that the estimated costs had escalated to €6 billion, and that completion of construction was delayed to 2016.[](#cite_note-neimagazine-110)

In December 2012, EDF announced that the estimated costs had escalated to €8.5 billion.[](#cite_note-lemonde-111) Also in December 2012, the Italian power company [Enel](/wiki/Enel "Enel") announced it was relinquishing its 12.5% stake in the project, and five future EPRs, so would be reimbursed its project stake of €613 million, plus interest.[](#cite_note-112)[](#cite_note-113)

In November 2014, EDF announced that completion of construction was delayed to 2017, due to delays in component delivery by Areva.[](#cite_note-ft-20141118-114)

In April 2015, Areva informed the French nuclear regulator ASN that anomalies had been detected in the reactor vessel steel, causing "lower than expected mechanical toughness values". Further tests are underway.[](#cite_note-wnn-20150407-115) In July 2015 [The Daily Telegraph](/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph "The Daily Telegraph") reported that Areva had been aware of this problem since 2006.[](#cite_note-telegraph-20150709-116) In June 2015, multiple faults in cooling system safety valves were discovered by ASN.[](#cite_note-telegraph-20150609-117) In September 2015, EDF announced that the estimated costs had escalated to €10.5 billion, and the start-up of the reactor was delayed to the fourth quarter of 2018.[](#cite_note-118)

In April 2016, ASN announced that additional weak spots had been found in the reactor steel, and Areva and EDF responded that new tests would be conducted, though construction work would continue.[](#cite_note-nei-20160412-119)

In February 2017, the [Financial Times](/wiki/Financial_Times "Financial Times") stated the project was six years late, and €7.2 billion over budget,[](#cite_note-ft-20170214-120) while renewed delays in the construction of the EPR-reactors at [Taishan Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant") prompted EDF to state that Flamanville 3 remains on schedule to start operations by the end of 2018, assuming it receives regulatory approval.[](#cite_note-121) In June 2017, the French regulator issued a provisional ruling that Flamanville 3 is safe to start.[](#cite_note-122)

The discovery of quality deviations in the welding led to a further revision of the schedule in July 2018. Fuel loading was delayed until the end of 2019, and the cost estimate was increased from €10.5 billion to €10.9 billion.[](#cite_note-123)

In June 2019, nuclear regulator ASN determined that eight welds in steam transfer pipes passing through the two wall containment, that EDF had hoped to repair after startup, must be repaired before the reactor is commissioned.[](#cite_note-124)[](#cite_note-wnn-20190620-125) By then, estimated costs were €11 billion.[](#cite_note-breteau-2022-126)

In October 2019, EDF announced that because of this issue costs would increase to €12.4 billion and that fuel loading would be delayed until the end of 2022.[](#cite_note-breteau-2022-126)[](#cite_note-wnn-20191009-127) Pierre Moscovici, president of the [Court of Audit](/wiki/Court_of_Audit_\(France\) "Court of Audit \(France\)"), gave a statement on 9 July 2020 concerning the release of the report on the delay costs of the Flamanville 3\. The report of the Court of Audit revealed that the costs could reach €19.1 billion instead of €12.4 billion when taking into account the additional charges due to the delay in construction.[](#cite_note-128)

In January 2022, it was announced that more time was needed for the repair of faulty welds and the solving of other issues.[](#cite_note-129)[](#cite_note-montel-20220112-130)[](#cite_note-breteau-2022-126) In December 2022, EDF announced a further delay of at least six months with an estimated cost increase of €500 million due to more work to establish a new process for the [stress relieving heat treatment](/wiki/Heat_treating#Stress_relieving "Heat treating") of some welds close to sensitive equipment. Fuel loading started in May 2024.[](#cite_note-131) Estimated total costs increased to €13.2 billion.,[](#cite_note-wnn-20221219-132) which would bring the specific costs to €8090 per kW net electric capacity. 

Flamanville 3 achieved first [criticality](/wiki/Criticality_\(status\) "Criticality \(status\)") in September 2024. Grid connection took place in December 2024.[](#cite_note-Flamanville-gridconnection-102)

In December 2025, following an authorization from the French nuclear safety and radiation protection authority ([ASNR](/wiki/Autorit%C3%A9_de_s%C3%BBret%C3%A9_nucl%C3%A9aire_et_de_radioprotection "Autorité de sûreté nucléaire et de radioprotection")), Flamanville 3 achieved 100% of its nuclear thermal power, outputting 1669 MW of gross electrical power.[](#cite_note-133)

### Taishan 1 and 2 (China)

See also: [Nuclear power in China](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_China "Nuclear power in China") and [Taishan Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant")

[](/wiki/File:Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant.jpg)[Taishan Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant") \- Units 1 & 2

In 2006, Areva took part in the first bidding process for the construction of four new nuclear reactors in China, together with Toshiba-owned [Westinghouse](/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_Company "Westinghouse Electric Company") and Russian [Atomstroyexport](/wiki/Atomstroyexport "Atomstroyexport").[](#cite_note-134) However Areva lost this bid in favour of Westinghouse's [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") reactors, in part because of Areva's refusal to transfer the expertise and knowledge to China.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Subsequently, Areva managed to win a deal in February 2007, worth about €8 billion ($10.5 billion) for two EPRs located in [Taishan](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant"), [Guangdong Province](/wiki/Guangdong "Guangdong") in southern China, in spite of sticking to its previous conditions.[](#cite_note-135)[](#cite_note-136) The [General Contractor](/wiki/General_Contractor "General Contractor") and [Operator](/wiki/Operator_\(profession\) "Operator \(profession\)") is the [China General Nuclear Power Group](/wiki/China_General_Nuclear_Power_Group "China General Nuclear Power Group") (CGN). 

The construction of the first reactor at [Taishan](/wiki/Taishan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Taishan Nuclear Power Plant") started officially on 18 November 2009, and the second on 15 April 2010.[](#cite_note-IAEA-PRIS-china-137) Construction of each unit was then planned to take 46 months, significantly faster and cheaper than the first two EPRs in Finland and France.[](#cite_note-138)

The [reactor pressure vessel](/wiki/Reactor_pressure_vessel "Reactor pressure vessel") of the first reactor was installed in June 2012,[](#cite_note-139) and the second in November 2014. The first pressure vessel had been imported from [Mitsubishi Heavy Industries](/wiki/Mitsubishi_Heavy_Industries "Mitsubishi Heavy Industries") in Japan, and [steam generators](/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\) "Steam generator \(nuclear power\)") from Areva in France. The second pressure vessel and associated steam generators had been made in China, by [Dongfang Electric](/wiki/Dongfang_Electric "Dongfang Electric") and [Shanghai Electric](/wiki/Shanghai_Electric "Shanghai Electric").[](#cite_note-wnn-20141105-140)

In 2014, construction was reported to be running over two years late, mainly due to key component delays and project management issues.[](#cite_note-nei-20150223-141)

Cold function tests were performed on Taishan 1 in February 2016, with start up expected in the first half of 2017. Taishan 2 was scheduled to start up later that year.[](#cite_note-wnn-20160201-142) However, commissioning dates were put back six months in February 2017, with commercial operation expected in the second half of 2017 and the first half of 2018.[](#cite_note-wnn-20170222-143)

In December 2017, Hong Kong media reported that a component had cracked during testing, needing to be replaced.[](#cite_note-crackinkey-144) In January 2018, commissioning was rescheduled again, with commercial operation expected in 2018 and 2019.[](#cite_note-wnn-20180102-145)

In June 2018, Taishan 1 achieved [criticality](/wiki/Criticality_\(status\) "Criticality \(status\)") for the first time.[](#cite_note-146) On 29 June 2018, Taishan 1 was connected to the grid.[](#cite_note-147)[](#cite_note-148) It entered commercial operation in December 2018.[](#cite_note-wnn-20181214-2)[](#cite_note-149) Taishan 2 reached these milestones in May 2019[](#cite_note-150) June 2019[](#cite_note-151) and September 2019,[](#cite_note-wnn-20190909-3) respectively. 

The Taishan project is led by Taishan Nuclear Power Joint Venture Co. (TNPJVC), a joint venture founded by CGN (51% ownership stake), EDF (30%), and Chinese utility Guangdong Energy Group (19%), also known as Yuedian.[](#cite_note-152)

Companies involved in supplying equipment to Taishan Unit 1 include Framatome, which manufactured the steam generators and pressuriser in France, and China's Dongfang Electric Corp. (DEC), which manufactured the Arabelle turbine in the engine room.[](#cite_note-153) That turbine was designed and licensed by General Electric. Other equipment suppliers for Unit 1 include Mitsubishi (reactor vessel); Škoda, a Czech company (core internals); and France's Jeumont Electric, which along with DEC provided primary pumps. 

In April 2020, Framatome signed a long-term service contract with TNPJVC to support operations of the two EPRs. This contract covers nuclear plant outage and maintenance work, including spare parts supply and engineering services for eight years.[](#cite_note-154)

In June 2021, higher than expected concentrations of radioactive gases were detected in the primary circuit of unit 1. This was later attributed to faulty fuel cladding.[](#cite_note-155)[](#cite_note-156) The reactor was taken offline in July 2021 and restarted in August 2022.[](#cite_note-157)

## Plants under construction

### Hinkley Point C (United Kingdom)

See also: [Nuclear power in the United Kingdom](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_United_Kingdom "Nuclear power in the United Kingdom") and [Hinkley Point C nuclear power station](/wiki/Hinkley_Point_C_nuclear_power_station "Hinkley Point C nuclear power station")

Hinkley Point C is a nuclear power station under construction with two EPR reactors and an electrical output of 3,200 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe") in [Somerset](/wiki/Somerset "Somerset"), England. 

The EPR underwent Generic Design Assessment by the [Office for Nuclear Regulation](/wiki/Office_for_Nuclear_Regulation "Office for Nuclear Regulation"), along with the Westinghouse [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000").[](#cite_note-158) Interim Design Acceptance Confirmations were postponed until lessons from the [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster "Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster") had been taken into account.[](#cite_note-159) EDF bought [British Energy](/wiki/British_Energy "British Energy") in 2009. EDF planned to build four new EPRs,[](#cite_note-160) subject to electricity pricing agreement with the government.[](#cite_note-161)[](#cite_note-telegraph-20130418-162) Areva has signed a strategic partnership with [Rolls-Royce](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_plc "Rolls-Royce plc") to support the build of EPRs.[](#cite_note-163) On 19 March 2013, the [Development Consent Order](/wiki/Development_Consent_Order "Development Consent Order") granting planning permission for Hinkley Point C was given,[](#cite_note-bbc-20130320-164) but negotiations with the UK government about electricity pricing, and project financing with private investors, still needed to be concluded.[](#cite_note-bbc-20130319-165)

On 21 October 2013, EDF Energy announced that an agreement had been reached regarding the nuclear stations to be built on the site of Hinkley Point C. EDF Group and the UK Government agreed on the key commercial terms of the investment contract. The final investment decision was conditional on completion of the remaining key steps, including the agreement of the [European Commission](/wiki/European_Commission "European Commission"). 

On 8 October 2014, the European Commission announced their agreement, with 16 out of 28 commissioners agreeing with the go ahead of the construction. On 21 September 2015, the British government announced it would provide a £2 billion support package for Hinkley Point C as Britain's first nuclear power station in 20 years.[](#cite_note-166)

On 21 October 2015, during Chinese [president Xi Jinping's](/wiki/Xi_Jinping "Xi Jinping") state visit to the United Kingdom, EDF and CGN signed an investment agreement for the £18 billion (€21.1 billion) project to build two reactors at Hinkley Point.[](#cite_note-167)[](#cite_note-guardian-20160309-168)

In June 2016, EDF managers told Members of Parliament that the Hinkley Point C proposal should be postponed, until it has "solved a litany of problems", including EDF's "soaring debts".[](#cite_note-169) On 28 July 2016, after the resignation of a board member, the EDF board approved the final investment decision for the project.[](#cite_note-guardian-20160728-170) However [Greg Clark](/wiki/Greg_Clark "Greg Clark"), the new [Secretary of State for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy](/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Business,_Energy_and_Industrial_Strategy "Secretary of State for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy") in the new [government of Theresa May](/wiki/First_May_ministry "First May ministry"), then announced that the government would not sign the contract over the next few days as expected, but delay the contract to autumn to "consider carefully all the component parts of this project".[](#cite_note-telegraph-20160728-171) Final government approval was given in September 2016.[](#cite_note-guardian-20160915-6)

In July 2017, following an internal review, EDF announced revised estimates for the scheme, which included at least £1.5 billion of additional costs and up to 15 months of additional programme, leading to updated total cost estimates of £19.6–20.3 billion.[](#cite_note-172)[](#cite_note-173)[](#cite_note-174) After a number of subsequent cost increases and delays,[](#cite_note-175)[](#cite_note-176)[](#cite_note-bbc-20220520-7) costs are now estimated to be between £31 and £35 billion (€36.3–41 billion), with the first unit estimated to start generating electricity around 2030.[](#cite_note-guardian-20240123-8)

The approximate 2,000 cubic metres (71,000 cu ft) concrete pour for the first reactor started on 11 December 2018. It was completed over a 30-hour period, creating the first part of the unit one 4,500 tonne base, a platform 3.2 metres (10 ft 6 in) thick. The reactor building will be built on the (to be completed) platform.[](#cite_note-177) This construction start marks the first new reactor build in the UK after a 30-year break, and the second PWR in the UK, after [Sizewell B](/wiki/Sizewell_nuclear_power_stations#Sizewell_B "Sizewell nuclear power stations"). 

Completion of the base for the first reactor, the final 8,954 cubic metres (316,200 cu ft) of concrete, was achieved in June 2019.[](#cite_note-178) Completion of the base for the second reactor, 8,991 cubic metres (317,500 cu ft) of concrete, was achieved in June 2020.[](#cite_note-179)

In February 2023, the first nuclear reactor [pressure vessel](/wiki/Pressure_vessel "Pressure vessel") was delivered to site via the [Bristol Channel](/wiki/Bristol_Channel "Bristol Channel") Hinkley-dedicated wharf at [Combwich](/wiki/Combwich "Combwich").[](#cite_note-pv23-180) The pressure vessel was built in France in 2022 by [Framatome](/wiki/Framatome "Framatome").[](#cite_note-181)

In January 2024, EDF presented three scenarios for the works, including when Unit 1 would become operational; with planned installation productivity a 2029 start costing £31 billion (2015 prices, £41.6 billion in 2024 prices), with less favourable installation productivity a 2030 start costing £34 billion (2015 prices, £46.5 billion in 2024 prices) or an unfavourable scenario with 2031 start costing £35 billion (2015 prices, £47.9 billion in 2024 prices).[](#cite_note-182)

In May 2024, the first of the 520-tonne [steam generators](/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\) "Steam generator \(nuclear power\)") was delivered to site in the same manner as the reactor pressure vessel.[](#cite_note-183)

In February 2026, EDF updated the timeline and costs, now expecting the first reactor to come online in 2030 and total costs to rise to £35 billion (2015 prices, more than £48 billion in 2026 prices), i.e. almost double the initial cost and 13 years after construction started.[](#cite_note-184) These costs are equivalent to more than €16,800 per kW net electric capacity. 

### Sizewell C (United Kingdom)

See also: [Sizewell C nuclear power station](/wiki/Sizewell_C_nuclear_power_station "Sizewell C nuclear power station")

The two EPR units at [Sizewell C](/wiki/Sizewell_C_nuclear_power_station "Sizewell C nuclear power station") received planning approval on 20 July 2022 and were granted a nuclear site licence on 7 May 2024.[](#cite_note-wnn-20180118-185)[](#cite_note-186)[](#cite_note-187) As of 2017, electricity production was expected to start in 2031 at the earliest.[](#cite_note-188) Site preparation work officially began on 15 January 2024.[](#cite_note-189)[](#cite_note-:2-190)

## Possible future power stations

### France

See also: [Nuclear power in France](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France "Nuclear power in France"), [Penly Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Penly_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Penly Nuclear Power Plant"), and [Gravelines Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Gravelines_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Gravelines Nuclear Power Plant")

In July 2008, the French President announced a second EPR would be built in France due to high oil and gas prices.[](#cite_note-191) [Penly](/wiki/Penly_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Penly Nuclear Power Plant") was chosen as the site in 2009, with construction planned to start in 2012.[](#cite_note-192) However, in 2011, following the [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster "Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster"), EDF postponed public consultations.[](#cite_note-193) In February 2013, the Minister of Industrial Renewal [Arnaud Montebourg](/wiki/Arnaud_Montebourg "Arnaud Montebourg") stated that the plans for a new EPR reactor at Penly had been cancelled, citing the capacity for electricity production and massive investments in [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy "Renewable energy") along with his confidence in the EPR as a competitive project in foreign countries.[](#cite_note-194)[](#cite_note-195)

Plans to build new reactors in France were later revived. [Penly](/wiki/Penly_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Penly Nuclear Power Plant") and [Gravelines](/wiki/Gravelines_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Gravelines Nuclear Power Plant") are among the candidates for the installation of a pair of EPR reactors.[](#cite_note-196)

In October 2019, newspaper _[Le Monde](/wiki/Le_Monde "Le Monde")_ reported that the French government had sent EDF a "mission letter" in which it asked the company to prepare to build a total of six EPR reactors across three sites in the next 15 years.[](#cite_note-reuters-20191014-197) A government decision on the construction of new reactors was not expected until after 2022.[](#cite_note-nei-20200114-41) EDF has submitted a proposal to build six EPR2s for around €50 billion.[](#cite_note-198) In February 2022, French president Emmanuel Macron announced that France would in fact build six new EPR2 reactors, the first to be commissioned by 2035, and with an option for eight more.[](#cite_note-199)[](#cite_note-200)

In June 2023, EDF announced it was starting the authorisation process to build two EPR2 reactors at [Penly Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Penly_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Penly Nuclear Power Plant"), anticipating that site preparatory work would begin in summer 2024 and construction would start about 2027.[](#cite_note-wnn-20230630-46) In 2024, the French government was considering providing EDF with interest-free reactor development loans and a long-term electricity price guarantee to support EPR2 builds.[](#cite_note-nei-20241129-201) Three dual reactor plants were estimated to cost €51.7 billion.[](#cite_note-wnn-20250905-202)[](#cite_note-edf-20230314-47)

In March 2024, media reported that expected costs for the six reactors had increased to €67.4 billion,[](#cite_note-wnn-20250905-202)[](#cite_note-203)[](#cite_note-204) equivalent to €6800 per kW net electric capacity. In January 2025, the [Court of Auditors](/wiki/Court_of_Auditors_\(France\) "Court of Auditors \(France\)") reported that France was "far from ready" to start building EPR2 reactors due to financing uncertainties and the unpreparedness of the construction supply chain.[](#cite_note-f24-20250114-205)

In March 2025, the Nuclear Policy Council agreed that a subsidised government loan, possibly zero-interest,[](#cite_note-f24-20250114-205) should be made available to cover at least half the build cost of six EPR2 reactors, though this will need European Union state-subsidy approval. A [Contract for Difference](/wiki/Contract_for_Difference#Electricity_generation "Contract for Difference") at no more than €100 per MWh would cover the remaining costs. However it delayed the planned commissioning of EPR2 reactors by three years to 2038, though the construction start target remained at 2027. The first three pairs of EPR2 reactors are proposed for the Penly, Gravelines and Bugey sites.[](#cite_note-nei-20250321-206)[](#cite_note-wnn-20250318-207) EDF is expected to make a final investment decision in 2026.[](#cite_note-wnn-20250905-202)

In December 2025, EDF reported that the cost for the six reactors was now €72.8 billion (2020 prices, ~€89 billion in 2025 prices), approved investing €2.7 billion in 2026, and were targeting 2038 for commissioning of the first reactor at Penly, with subsequent reactors following "at intervals of 12 to 18 months".[](#cite_note-208) These estimates are equivalent to €7,300 (2020 prices, ~€8,900 in 2025 prices) per kW net electric capacity. 

### India

In February 2009, the [Nuclear Power Corporation of India](/wiki/Nuclear_Power_Corporation_of_India "Nuclear Power Corporation of India") (NPCIL) signed a memorandum of understanding with Areva to set up two EPR reactors at [Jaitapur](/wiki/Jaitapur_Nuclear_Power_Project "Jaitapur Nuclear Power Project") in Maharashtra. This was followed by a framework agreement in December 2010.[](#cite_note-209)

In January 2016, during French president [François Hollande](/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Hollande "François Hollande")'s state visit to India a joint statement with Indian Prime Minister [Narendra Modi](/wiki/Narendra_Modi "Narendra Modi") was issued. According to the statement the two leaders "have agreed on a roadmap of cooperation to speed up discussions on the Jaitapur project".[](#cite_note-wnn-201690125-210)

In March 2018, an Industrial Way Forward Agreement between EDF and NPCIL was signed, with an objective of producing a tender for six reactors.[](#cite_note-wnn-20180312-211)[](#cite_note-wnn-20180627-212)

In April 2021, EDF submitted to NPCIL an offer to develop six EPR reactors at the Jaitapur site, with a combined installed capacity of 9.6 GWe.[](#cite_note-wnn-20210423-213)

## Unsuccessful proposals

### Canada

See also: [Nuclear power in Canada](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Canada "Nuclear power in Canada")

EPR was considered for the two (possible expansion to four) reactor addition to the [Darlington Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station") in [Ontario, Canada](/wiki/Ontario,_Canada "Ontario, Canada"). However, the official bids had to include all contingencies, and Areva failed to enter a final bid meeting these requirements. The project was ultimately abandoned when the only bid, made by Canada's [AECL](/wiki/AECL "AECL"), came in at well over $10/Wp.[](#cite_note-216)

EPR was briefly considered for an installation in [New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick "New Brunswick"), replacing or supplanting that province's single [CANDU 6](/wiki/CANDU_reactor "CANDU reactor"). These plans lasted only from June 2010 until an election two months later, when the plan immediately disappeared from further study.[](#cite_note-217)

### Czech Republic

See also: [Nuclear power in the Czech Republic](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_Czech_Republic "Nuclear power in the Czech Republic")

In October 2012, Czech utility company [ČEZ](/wiki/%C4%8CEZ_Group "ČEZ Group") announced that Areva was eliminated from a tender for the construction of two reactors for [Temelín nuclear plant](/wiki/Temel%C3%ADn_Nuclear_Power_Station "Temelín Nuclear Power Station"). Areva failed to comply with legal requirements of the tender.[](#cite_note-218) In April 2014, ČEZ cancelled the tender, because of low power prices and the government's refusal to support a minimum guaranteed energy price.[](#cite_note-219)

In June 2021, the Czech Ministry of Industry and Trade invited EDF, along with [Westinghouse](/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_Company "Westinghouse Electric Company") and [Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power](/wiki/Korea_Hydro_%26_Nuclear_Power "Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power") (KHNP) to participate in a pre-qualification round for a new unit at the [Dukovany Nuclear Power Station](/wiki/Dukovany_Nuclear_Power_Station "Dukovany Nuclear Power Station").[](#cite_note-220) EDF was proposing the 1200 MWe version of the EPR (EPR-1200) for the project.[](#cite_note-221) In February 2024, the tender was changed to binding offers for up to four new units. In July 2024, KHNP was selected as the preferred bidder.[](#cite_note-222)

### Finland

See also: [Nuclear power in Finland](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Finland "Nuclear power in Finland")

In 2010, the Finnish parliament decided to allow two new reactors. Both [TVO](/wiki/Teollisuuden_Voima "Teollisuuden Voima") and [Fennovoima](/wiki/Fennovoima "Fennovoima") were considering the EPR.[](#cite_note-223)[](#cite_note-224) In December 2013, Fennovoima confirmed it had selected a Russian AES-2006 [VVER](/wiki/VVER "VVER") pressurised water reactor in preference to the EPR.[](#cite_note-225) In May 2022, after significant delays in the design and licensing phase of the project and in light of the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine"), Fennovoima cancelled the contract with [Rosatom](/wiki/Rosatom "Rosatom") to build the power plant.[](#cite_note-226)

### Italy

See also: [Nuclear power in Italy](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Italy "Nuclear power in Italy")

On 24 February 2009, Italy and France agreed to study the feasibility of building four new nuclear power stations in Italy.[](#cite_note-227) Following this, on 3 August 2009, EDF and [Enel](/wiki/Enel "Enel") established a joint venture, Sviluppo Nucleare Italia, to study the feasibility of building at least four EPRs.[](#cite_note-228)

However, in the [2011 referendum](/wiki/Italian_referendum,_2011 "Italian referendum, 2011"), soon after the [Fukushima nuclear disaster](/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_disaster "Fukushima nuclear disaster"), Italians voted to [repeal](/wiki/Repeal "Repeal") the new regulations permitting nuclear power in Italy. Abrogation of laws is put in effect when at least 50%+1 electors make a valid vote and a majority of these voters are in favour of abrogation. In this referendum, there was a 55% valid [voter turnout](/wiki/Voter_turnout "Voter turnout") and 94% voted to abrogate the new regulations. 

### Poland

See also: [Nuclear power in Poland](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Poland "Nuclear power in Poland")

In October 2021, EDF made an offer to Poland to build four or six EPR reactors across two to three sites. The combined installed capacity of the reactors would be either 6.6 or 9.9 GWe.[](#cite_note-229)

In October 2022, Poland selected Westinghouse's [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") design, with construction of the first three-unit plant expected to start in 2026.[](#cite_note-230)

### United Arab Emirates

See also: [Nuclear power in the United Arab Emirates](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_United_Arab_Emirates "Nuclear power in the United Arab Emirates")

In March 2008, French president [Nicolas Sarkozy](/wiki/Nicolas_Sarkozy "Nicolas Sarkozy") reached an agreement with the UAE cabinet that "outlines a cooperation framework for the assessment and possible use of nuclear energy for peaceful ends". This agreement was not a contract for EPR construction by any of the French nuclear companies, [Total S.A.](/wiki/Total_S.A. "Total S.A."), [Suez](/wiki/Suez_\(company,_1997%E2%80%932008\) "Suez \(company, 1997–2008\)") or Areva.[](#cite_note-231)

In May 2009, US President [Barack Obama](/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama") signed a [similar agreement](/wiki/U.S.%E2%80%93UAE_123_Agreement_for_Peaceful_Civilian_Nuclear_Energy_Cooperation "U.S.–UAE 123 Agreement for Peaceful Civilian Nuclear Energy Cooperation") with the UAE. Contracts for reactors were not given, nor was there any guarantee made that US companies would receive them.[](#cite_note-232)

In December 2009, the [United Arab Emirates](/wiki/United_Arab_Emirates "United Arab Emirates") declined both the American and French bids and awarded a contract for construction of four non-EPR stations ([APR-1400](/wiki/APR-1400 "APR-1400")) to a South Korean group including [Korea Electric Power Corporation](/wiki/Korea_Electric_Power_Corporation "Korea Electric Power Corporation"), [Hyundai Engineering and Construction](/wiki/Hyundai_Engineering_and_Construction "Hyundai Engineering and Construction"), [Samsung](/wiki/Samsung "Samsung") and [Doosan Heavy Industries](/wiki/Doosan_Heavy_Industries "Doosan Heavy Industries").[](#cite_note-233)

After losing this order, Areva considered whether it should reintroduce the marketing of a smaller and simpler second-generation reactor design alongside the EPR, for countries that are new to nuclear power.[](#cite_note-ft-20100115-234) As of 2011 Areva and [Mitsubishi Heavy Industries](/wiki/Mitsubishi_Heavy_Industries "Mitsubishi Heavy Industries") offer a smaller 1100 MWe [ATMEA1](/wiki/Atmea "Atmea") Generation III PWR.[](#cite_note-235)

### United States

See also: [Nuclear power in the United States](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_the_United_States "Nuclear power in the United States")

The US-EPR, the version of the EPR submitted to the U.S. regulator,[](#cite_note-wnn-20150306-236) is one of the competitors for the next generation of nuclear stations in the United States, along with the [AP1000](/wiki/AP1000 "AP1000") and the [ESBWR](/wiki/ESBWR "ESBWR"). In February 2015, Areva asked to suspend the Design Certification Application Review process at the U.S. [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Nuclear_Regulatory_Commission "Nuclear Regulatory Commission") (NRC).[](#cite_note-wnn-20150306-236) It had been under review there with expectation to submit an application for final design approval and standard design certification since 14 December 2007.[](#cite_note-237) UniStar, Amarillo Power, PPL Corp and [AmerenUE](/wiki/AmerenUE "AmerenUE") announced plans to file a [Combined Construction and Operating License](/wiki/Combined_Construction_and_Operating_License "Combined Construction and Operating License") application in 2008 for the US-EPR at its [Callaway](/wiki/Callaway_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Callaway Nuclear Generating Station") station. UniStar filed a partial application in July 2007 for a proposed third unit at the [Calvert Cliffs Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Calvert_Cliffs_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Calvert Cliffs Nuclear Power Plant") in Maryland. However, both proposals were subsequently cancelled. 

In April 2009, Missouri legislators balked at preconstruction rate increases, prompting AmerenUE to suspend plans for its reactor.[](#cite_note-238) In July 2010, Constellation Energy Group cut spending on UniStar for the Calvert Cliffs Nuclear Power Station because of uncertainties for a loan guarantee from the U.S. Department of Energy,[](#cite_note-nyt-20100730-239)[](#cite_note-240) and subsequently pulled out of the project.[](#cite_note-ft-20101019-241) In October 2008, Areva announced that it would partner with US defense firm [Northrop Grumman](/wiki/Northrop_Grumman "Northrop Grumman") to establish a $380 million facility to construct modules and assemblies for the EPR and US-EPR reactors at Northrop Grumman's [Newport News Shipyard](/wiki/Northrop_Grumman_Shipbuilding "Northrop Grumman Shipbuilding") in [Virginia](/wiki/Virginia "Virginia").[](#cite_note-242)[](#cite_note-243) The project was suspended indefinitely in May 2011.[](#cite_note-244)
