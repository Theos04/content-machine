Propulsion system for marine vessels utilizing a nuclear powerplant

[](/wiki/File:50_Let_Pobedy.jpg)When the nuclear-powered [_Arktika_ class](/wiki/Arktika-class_icebreaker "Arktika-class icebreaker") _[50 Let Pobedy](/wiki/50_Let_Pobedy "50 Let Pobedy")_ was put into service in 2007, it became the world's largest [icebreaker](/wiki/Icebreaker "Icebreaker").

**Nuclear marine propulsion** is [propulsion of a ship](/wiki/Marine_propulsion "Marine propulsion") or submarine with heat provided by a [nuclear reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor"). The power plant heats water to produce steam for a turbine used to turn the ship's [propeller](/wiki/Propeller "Propeller") through a [gearbox](/wiki/Transmission_\(mechanics\) "Transmission \(mechanics\)") or through an electric generator and motor. Nuclear propulsion is used primarily within naval warships such as [nuclear submarines](/wiki/Nuclear_submarines "Nuclear submarines") and [supercarriers](/wiki/Supercarrier "Supercarrier"). A small number of experimental civil nuclear ships have been built.[](#cite_note-1)

Compared to oil- or coal-fuelled ships, nuclear propulsion offers the advantage of very long intervals of operation before refueling. All the fuel is contained within the nuclear reactor, so no cargo or supplies space is taken up by fuel, nor is space taken up by exhaust stacks or combustion air intakes.[](#cite_note-auto1-2) The low fuel cost is offset by high operating costs and investment in infrastructure, however, so nearly all nuclear-powered vessels are military.[](#cite_note-auto1-2)

## Power plants

### Basic operation of naval ship or submarine

Most naval nuclear reactors are of the [pressurized water](/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor "Pressurized water reactor") type, with the exception of a few[_[quantify](/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_] attempts[_[by whom?](/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")_] at using liquid sodium-cooled reactors.[](#cite_note-auto1-2) A primary water circuit transfers heat generated from [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") in the fuel to a [steam generator](/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\) "Steam generator \(nuclear power\)"); this water is kept under pressure so it does not boil. This circuit operates at a temperature of around 250 to 300 °C (482 to 572 °F). Any [radioactive contamination](/wiki/Radioactive_contamination "Radioactive contamination") in the primary water is confined. Water is circulated by pumps; at lower power levels, reactors designed for submarines may rely on natural circulation of the water to reduce noise generated by the pumps.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The hot water from the reactor heats a separate water circuit in the steam generator. That water is converted to steam and passes through [steam driers](/wiki/Steam_drier "Steam drier") on its way to the [steam turbine](/wiki/Steam_turbine "Steam turbine"). Spent steam at low pressure runs through a [condenser](/wiki/Condenser_\(heat_transfer\) "Condenser \(heat transfer\)") cooled by seawater and returns to liquid form. The water is pumped back to the steam generator and continues the cycle. Any water lost in the process can be made up by [desalinated](/wiki/Desalination "Desalination") sea water added to the steam generator feed water.[](#cite_note-3)

In the turbine, the steam expands and reduces its pressure as it imparts energy to the rotating blades of the turbine. There may be many stages of rotating blades and fixed guide vanes. The output shaft of the turbine may be connected to a gearbox to reduce rotation speed, then a shaft connects to the vessel's propellers. In another form of drive system, the turbine turns an electrical generator, and the electric power produced is fed to one or more drive motors for the vessel's propellers. The [Russian](/wiki/Russian_Navy "Russian Navy"), [United States](/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy"), and [British](/wiki/Royal_Navy "Royal Navy") navies rely on direct steam turbine propulsion, while French and Chinese ships use the turbine to generate electricity for propulsion ([turbo-electric transmission](/wiki/Turbo-electric_transmission "Turbo-electric transmission")).[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Some nuclear submarines have a single reactor, but Russian submarines have two, and so had [USS  _Triton_](/wiki/USS_Triton_\(SSRN-586\) "USS Triton \(SSRN-586\)"). Most American aircraft carriers are powered by two reactors, but [USS  _Enterprise_](/wiki/USS_Enterprise_\(CVN-65\) "USS Enterprise \(CVN-65\)") had eight. The majority of marine [reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") are of the [pressurized water](/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor "Pressurized water reactor") type, although the U.S. and Soviet navies have designed warships powered with [liquid metal cooled reactors](/wiki/Liquid_metal_cooled_reactor "Liquid metal cooled reactor").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

### Differences from land power plants

Marine-type reactors differ from land-based commercial electric power reactors in several respects.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

While land-based reactors in nuclear power plants produce up to around 1600 megawatts of net electrical power (the [nameplate capacity](/wiki/Nameplate_capacity "Nameplate capacity") of the [EPR](/wiki/EPR_\(nuclear_reactor\) "EPR \(nuclear reactor\)")), a typical marine propulsion reactor produces no more than a few hundred megawatts. Some [small modular reactors](/wiki/Small_modular_reactor "Small modular reactor") (SMR) are similar to marine propulsion reactors in capacity and some design considerations and thus nuclear marine propulsion (whether civilian or military) is sometimes proposed as an additional market niche for SMRs. Unlike for land-based applications where hundreds of hectares can be occupied by installations like [Bruce Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Bruce_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Bruce Nuclear Generating Station"), at sea tight space limits dictate that a marine reactor must be physically small, so it must generate higher power per unit of space. This means its components are subject to greater stresses than those of a land-based reactor. Its mechanical systems must operate flawlessly under the adverse conditions encountered at sea, including vibration and the pitching and rolling of a ship operating in rough seas. Reactor shutdown mechanisms cannot rely on gravity to drop control rods into place as in a land-based reactor that always remains upright. Salt water corrosion is an additional problem that complicates maintenance.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

[](/wiki/File:Nuclear_fuel_element.jpg)A nuclear fuel element for the cargo ship [NS  _Savannah_](/wiki/NS_Savannah "NS Savannah"). The element contains four bundles of 41 fuel rods. The uranium oxide is enriched to 4.2 and 4.6 percent U-235

As the core of a seagoing reactor is much smaller than a power reactor, the probability of a neutron intersecting with a fissionable nucleus before it escapes into the shielding is much lower. As such, the fuel is typically more highly enriched (i.e., contains a higher concentration of 235U vs. 238U) than that used in a land-based nuclear power plant, which increases the probability of fission to the level where a sustained reaction can occur. Some marine reactors run on relatively low-enriched [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium"), which requires more frequent refueling. Others run on [highly enriched uranium](/wiki/Highly_enriched_uranium "Highly enriched uranium"), varying from 20% 235U, to the over 96% 235U found in U.S. [submarines](/wiki/Submarine "Submarine"),[](#cite_note-4) in which the resulting smaller core is quieter in operation (a big advantage to a submarine).[](#cite_note-5) Using more-highly enriched fuel also increases the reactor's power density and extends the usable life of the nuclear fuel load, but is more expensive and a greater risk to [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") than less-highly enriched fuel.[](#cite_note-ANTIENRICHED-6)

A marine nuclear propulsion plant must be designed to be highly reliable and self-sufficient, requiring minimal maintenance and repairs, which might have to be undertaken many thousands of miles from its home port. One of the technical difficulties in designing fuel elements for a seagoing nuclear reactor is the creation of fuel elements that will withstand a large amount of radiation damage. Fuel elements may crack over time and gas bubbles may form. The fuel used in marine reactors is a metal-[zirconium](/wiki/Zirconium "Zirconium") alloy rather than the ceramic UO2 ([uranium dioxide](/wiki/Uranium_dioxide "Uranium dioxide")) often used in land-based reactors. Marine reactors are designed for long core life, enabled by the relatively high enrichment of the uranium and by incorporating a "[burnable poison](/wiki/Neutron_poison#Burnable_poisons "Neutron poison")" in the fuel elements, which is slowly depleted as the fuel elements age and become less reactive. The gradual dissipation of the "nuclear poison" increases the reactivity of the core to compensate for the lessening reactivity of the aging fuel elements, thereby extending the usable life of the fuel. The compact [reactor pressure vessel](/wiki/Reactor_vessel "Reactor vessel") is provided with an internal [neutron](/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") shield, which reduces the [damage to the steel from constant neutron bombardment](/wiki/Neutron_embrittlement "Neutron embrittlement").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## Decommissioning

Decommissioning nuclear-powered submarines has become a major task for U.S. and Russian navies.[](#cite_note-7) After defuelling, U.S. practice is to cut the reactor section from the vessel for disposal in shallow land burial as low-level waste (see the _[ship-submarine recycling program](/wiki/Ship-submarine_recycling_program "Ship-submarine recycling program")_).[](#cite_note-8) In Russia, whole vessels, or sealed reactor sections, typically remain stored afloat, although a new facility near [Sayda Bay](/wiki/Sayda_Bay "Sayda Bay") is to provide storage in a concrete-floored facility on land for some submarines in the far north.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## Future designs

Russia built a [floating nuclear power plant](/wiki/Russian_floating_nuclear_power_station "Russian floating nuclear power station") for its far eastern territories. The design has two 35 MWe units based on the [KLT-40 reactor](/wiki/KLT-40_reactor "KLT-40 reactor") used in [icebreakers](/wiki/Icebreaker "Icebreaker") (with refueling every four years). Some Russian naval vessels have been used to supply electricity for domestic and industrial use in remote far eastern and Siberian towns.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

In 2010, [Lloyd's Register](/wiki/Lloyd%27s_Register "Lloyd's Register") was investigating the possibility of civilian nuclear marine propulsion and rewriting draft rules (see text under _Merchant Ships_).[](#cite_note-wnnLL-9)[](#cite_note-Hirdaris_101–130-10)[](#cite_note-Hirdaris_A37-A60-11)

## Civil liability

Insurance of nuclear vessels is not like the insurance of conventional ships. The consequences of an accident could span national boundaries, and the magnitude of possible damage is beyond the capacity of private insurers.[](#cite_note-12) A special international agreement, the _Brussels Convention on the Liability of Operators of Nuclear Ships_ , developed in 1962, would have made signatory national governments liable for accidents caused by nuclear vessels under their flag[](#cite_note-13) but was never ratified owing to disagreement on the inclusion of warships under the convention.[](#cite_note-14) Nuclear reactors under United States jurisdiction are insured by the provisions of the [Price–Anderson Act](/wiki/Price%E2%80%93Anderson_Nuclear_Industries_Indemnity_Act "Price–Anderson Nuclear Industries Indemnity Act").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

## Military nuclear ships

[](/wiki/File:USS_Nimitz_\(CVN-68\)_with_California_\(CGN-36\)_and_South_Carolina_\(CGN-37\)_c1976.jpg)In addition to nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, the United States once operated nuclear-powered cruisers.

By 1990, there were more nuclear reactors powering ships (mostly military) than there were generating electric power in commercial power plants worldwide.[](#cite_note-15)

Under the direction of [U.S. Navy](/wiki/U.S._Navy "U.S. Navy") Captain (later Admiral) [Hyman G. Rickover](/wiki/Hyman_G._Rickover "Hyman G. Rickover"),[](#cite_note-16) the design, development and production of nuclear marine propulsion plants started in the [United States](/wiki/United_States "United States") in the 1940s. The first prototype naval reactor was constructed and tested at the [Naval Reactor Facility](/wiki/Naval_Reactor_Facility "Naval Reactor Facility") at the National Reactor Testing Station in Idaho (now called the [Idaho National Laboratory](/wiki/Idaho_National_Laboratory "Idaho National Laboratory")) in 1953. 

### Submarines

Further information: [Nuclear submarine](/wiki/Nuclear_submarine "Nuclear submarine")

[](/wiki/File:FS_Saphir_06.jpg)The nuclear-propelled [French submarine  _Saphir_](/wiki/French_submarine_Saphir_\(S602\) "French submarine Saphir \(S602\)") returning to [Toulon](/wiki/Toulon "Toulon"), its [home port](/wiki/Home_port "Home port"), after [Mission Héraclès](/wiki/Mission_H%C3%A9racl%C3%A8s "Mission Héraclès")

The first [nuclear submarine](/wiki/Nuclear_submarine "Nuclear submarine"), [USS  _Nautilus_ (SSN-571)](/wiki/USS_Nautilus_\(SSN-571\) "USS Nautilus \(SSN-571\)"), put to sea in 1955 (SS was a traditional [hull classification symbol](/wiki/Hull_classification_symbol "Hull classification symbol") for U.S. submarines, while SSN denoted the first "nuclear" submarine).[](#cite_note-17)

The Soviet Union also developed nuclear submarines. The first types developed were the Project 627, NATO-designated [November class](/wiki/November-class_submarine "November-class submarine") with two water-cooled reactors, the first of which, K-3 _Leninsky Komsomol_ , was underway under nuclear power in 1958.[](#cite_note-18)

Nuclear power revolutionized the submarine, finally making it a true "underwater" vessel, rather than a "submersible" craft, which could only stay underwater for limited periods. It gave the submarine the ability to operate submerged at high speeds, comparable to those of surface vessels, for unlimited periods, dependent only on the endurance of its crew. To demonstrate this [USS  _Triton_](/wiki/USS_Triton_\(SSRN-586\) "USS Triton \(SSRN-586\)") was the first vessel to execute a submerged [circumnavigation](/wiki/Circumnavigation "Circumnavigation") of the Earth ([Operation Sandblast](/wiki/Operation_Sandblast "Operation Sandblast")), doing so in 1960.[](#cite_note-19)

_Nautilus_ , with a [pressurized water reactor](/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor "Pressurized water reactor") (PWR), led to the parallel development of other submarines like a unique liquid metal cooled (sodium) reactor in [USS  _Seawolf_](/wiki/USS_Seawolf_\(SSN-575\) "USS Seawolf \(SSN-575\)"), or two reactors in _Triton_ , and then the [_Skate_ -class](/wiki/Skate-class_submarine "Skate-class submarine") submarines, powered by single reactors, and a cruiser, [USS  _Long Beach_](/wiki/USS_Long_Beach_\(CGN-9\) "USS Long Beach \(CGN-9\)"), in 1961, powered by two reactors.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

By 1962, the [United States Navy](/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy") had 26 operational nuclear submarines and another 30 under construction. Nuclear power had revolutionized the Navy. The United States shared its technology with the [United Kingdom](/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom"), while [French](/wiki/France "France"), [Soviet](/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union"), [Indian](/wiki/India "India") and [Chinese](/wiki/China "China") development proceeded separately.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

After the _Skate_ -class vessels, U.S. submarines were powered by a series of standardized, single-reactor designs built by [Westinghouse](/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_Corporation "Westinghouse Electric Corporation") and [General Electric](/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric"). [Rolls-Royce plc](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_plc "Rolls-Royce plc") built similar units for [Royal Navy](/wiki/Royal_Navy "Royal Navy") submarines, eventually developing a modified version of their own, the [PWR2](/wiki/Rolls-Royce_PWR#PWR2 "Rolls-Royce PWR").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The largest nuclear submarines ever built are the 26,500 tonne Russian [Typhoon class](/wiki/Typhoon-class_submarine "Typhoon-class submarine"). The smallest nuclear warships to date are the 2,700 tonne French [_Rubis_ -class](/wiki/Rubis-class_submarine "Rubis-class submarine") attack submarines. The U.S. Navy operated an unarmed nuclear submarine, the [NR-1 Deep Submergence Craft](/wiki/NR-1_Deep_Submergence_Craft "NR-1 Deep Submergence Craft"), between 1969 and 2008, which was not a combat vessel but was the smallest nuclear-powered submarine at 400 tons.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The _Kirov_ class, Soviet designation 'Project 1144 Orlan' ([sea eagle](/wiki/Sea_eagle "Sea eagle")), is a class of [nuclear-powered](/wiki/Nuclear_powered "Nuclear powered") [guided-missile cruisers](/wiki/Guided_missile_cruiser "Guided missile cruiser") of the [Soviet Navy](/wiki/Soviet_Navy "Soviet Navy") and [Russian Navy](/wiki/Russian_Navy "Russian Navy"), the largest and heaviest [surface combatant](/wiki/Surface_combatant "Surface combatant") warships (i.e. not an [aircraft carrier](/wiki/Aircraft_carrier "Aircraft carrier"), [amphibious assault ship](/wiki/Amphibious_assault_ship "Amphibious assault ship"), or [submarine](/wiki/Submarine "Submarine")) in operation in the world. The Soviet classification of the ship-type is "heavy nuclear-powered guided missile cruiser" ([Russian](/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): тяжёлый атомный ракетный крейсер). The ships are often referred to as [battlecruisers](/wiki/Battlecruiser "Battlecruiser") by Western defence commentators due to their size and general appearance.[](#cite_note-24)

## Civilian nuclear ships

[](/wiki/File:Ns-savanna-eng.jpg)Engineer epaulette from _Savannah_

The following are [ships](/wiki/Ship "Ship") that are or were in commercial or civilian use and have nuclear marine propulsion. 

### Merchant ships

Nuclear-powered civil merchant ships have not developed beyond a few experimental ships. The U.S.-built [NS  _Savannah_](/wiki/NS_Savannah "NS Savannah"), completed in 1962, was primarily a demonstration of civil nuclear power and was too small and expensive to operate economically as a merchant ship. The design was too much of a compromise, being neither an efficient freighter nor a viable passenger liner. The German-built [_Otto Hahn_](/wiki/Otto_Hahn_\(ship\) "Otto Hahn \(ship\)"), a cargo ship and research facility completed in 1968, sailed some 650,000 nautical miles (1,200,000 km) on 126 voyages over 10 years without any technical problems.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] It proved too expensive to operate and was converted to diesel. The Japanese [_Mutsu_](/wiki/RV_Mirai "RV Mirai"), completed in 1972, was dogged by technical and political problems. Its reactor had significant radiation leakage and fishermen protested against the vessel's operation. All of these three ships used [low-enriched uranium](/wiki/Enriched_uranium#Low-enriched_uranium_\(LEU\) "Enriched uranium"). _[Sevmorput](/wiki/Sevmorput "Sevmorput")_ , a Soviet and later Russian [LASH carrier](/wiki/Lighter_aboard_ship "Lighter aboard ship") with icebreaking capability, has operated successfully on the [Northern Sea Route](/wiki/Northern_Sea_Route "Northern Sea Route") since it was commissioned in 1988. As of 2021[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Nuclear_marine_propulsion&action=edit), it is the only nuclear-powered merchant ship in service.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Civilian nuclear ships suffer from the costs of specialized infrastructure. The _Savannah_ was expensive to operate since it was the only vessel using its specialized nuclear shore staff and servicing facility. A larger fleet could share fixed costs among more operating vessels, reducing operating costs. 

Despite this, there is still interest in nuclear propulsion. In November 2010 British Maritime Technology and Lloyd's Register embarked upon a two-year study with U.S.-based Hyperion Power Generation (now [Gen4 Energy](/wiki/Gen4_Energy "Gen4 Energy")), and the Greek ship operator Enterprises Shipping and Trading SA to investigate the practical maritime applications for small modular reactors. The research intended to produce a concept tanker-ship design, based on a 70 MWt reactor such as Hyperion's. In response to its members' interest in nuclear propulsion, Lloyd's Register has also re-written its 'rules' for nuclear ships, which concern the integration of a reactor certified by a land-based regulator with the rest of the ship. The overall rationale of the rule-making process assumes that in contrast to the current marine industry practice where the designer/builder typically demonstrates compliance with regulatory requirements, in the future the nuclear regulators will wish to ensure that it is the operator of the nuclear plant that demonstrates safety in operation, in addition to the safety through design and construction. Nuclear ships are currently the responsibility of their own countries, but none are involved in international trade. As a result of this work in 2014 two papers on commercial nuclear marine propulsion were published by Lloyd's Register and the other members of this consortium.[](#cite_note-Hirdaris_101–130-10)[](#cite_note-Hirdaris_A37-A60-11) These publications review past and recent work in the area of marine nuclear propulsion and describe a preliminary concept design study for a 155,000 [DWT](/wiki/Deadweight_tonnage "Deadweight tonnage") Suezmax tanker that is based on a conventional hull form with alternative arrangements for accommodating a 70 MWt nuclear propulsion plant delivering up to 23.5 MW shaft power at maximum continuous rating (average: 9.75 MW). The Gen4Energy power module is considered. This is a small fast-neutron reactor using lead–bismuth eutectic cooling and able to operate for ten full-power years before refueling, and in service last for a 25-year operational life of the vessel. They conclude that the concept is feasible, but further maturity of nuclear technology and the development and harmonisation of the regulatory framework would be necessary before the concept would be viable.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Nuclear propulsion has been proposed again on the wave of [decarbonization](/wiki/Low-carbon_economy "Low-carbon economy") of marine shipping, which accounts for 3–4% of global greenhouse gas emissions.[](#cite_note-31)

### Icebreakers

Main article: [Nuclear-powered icebreaker](/wiki/Nuclear-powered_icebreaker "Nuclear-powered icebreaker")

Nuclear propulsion has proven both technically and economically feasible for [nuclear-powered icebreakers](/wiki/Nuclear-powered_icebreaker "Nuclear-powered icebreaker") in the [Soviet](/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union"), and later [Russian](/wiki/Russia "Russia"), [Arctic](/wiki/Arctic "Arctic"). Nuclear-fuelled ships operate for years without refueling, and the vessels have powerful engines, well-suited to the task of icebreaking.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

The [Soviet icebreaker _Lenin_](/wiki/Lenin_\(nuclear_icebreaker\) "Lenin \(nuclear icebreaker\)") was the world's first nuclear-powered surface vessel in 1959 and remained in service for 30 years (new reactors were fitted in 1970). It led to a series of larger icebreakers, the 23,500 [ton](/wiki/Tonnage "Tonnage") [_Arktika_ class](/wiki/Arktika-class_icebreaker "Arktika-class icebreaker") of six vessels, launched beginning in 1975. These vessels have two reactors and are used in deep Arctic waters. [NS _Arktika_](/wiki/Arktika_\(1972_nuclear_icebreaker\) "Arktika \(1972 nuclear icebreaker\)") was the first surface vessel to reach the [North Pole](/wiki/North_Pole "North Pole").[](#cite_note-33)

For use in shallow waters such as estuaries and rivers, shallow-draft, [_Taymyr_ -class icebreakers](/wiki/Nuclear-powered_icebreaker#Taymyr_class "Nuclear-powered icebreaker") were built in [Finland](/wiki/Finland "Finland") and then fitted with their single-reactor nuclear propulsion system in [Russia](/wiki/Russia "Russia"). They were built to conform to international safety standards for nuclear vessels.[](#cite_note-34)

All nuclear-powered icebreakers have been commissioned by the Soviet Union or Russia.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
