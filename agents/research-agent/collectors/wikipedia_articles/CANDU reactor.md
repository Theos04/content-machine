Canadian heavy water nuclear reactor design

[](/wiki/File:CANDU_at_Qinshan.jpg)[Qinshan](/wiki/Qinshan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Qinshan Nuclear Power Plant") Phase III units 1 and 2, located in [Zhejiang](/wiki/Zhejiang "Zhejiang"), China.

The **CANDU** (**Canada deuterium uranium**) is a Canadian [pressurized heavy-water reactor](/wiki/Pressurized_heavy-water_reactor "Pressurized heavy-water reactor") design used to generate electric power.[](#cite_note-:0-1) The acronym refers to its [deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium") oxide ([heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water")) [moderator](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") and its use of (originally, [natural](/wiki/Natural_uranium "Natural uranium")) [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium") fuel. CANDU reactors were first developed in the late 1950s and 1960s by a partnership between [Atomic Energy of Canada Limited](/wiki/Atomic_Energy_of_Canada_Limited "Atomic Energy of Canada Limited") (AECL), the [Hydro-Electric Power Commission of Ontario](/wiki/Hydro-Electric_Power_Commission_of_Ontario "Hydro-Electric Power Commission of Ontario"), [Canadian General Electric](/wiki/Canadian_General_Electric "Canadian General Electric"), and other companies. It was later exported, and in 2026 there were 26 operational CANDU reactors worldwide: 17 in [Canada](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Canada "Nuclear power in Canada"), three in [South Korea](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_South_Korea "Nuclear power in South Korea"), two each in [Romania](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Romania "Nuclear power in Romania") and [China](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_China "Nuclear power in China"), and one each in [India](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_India "Nuclear power in India") and [Argentina](/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Argentina "Nuclear power in Argentina"). 

There have been two major types of CANDU reactors, the original design of around 500 [MWe](/wiki/Watt#MWe "Watt") that was intended to be used in multi-reactor installations in large plants, and the optimized **CANDU 6** in the 600 MWe class that is designed to be used in single stand-alone units or in small multi-unit plants. CANDU 6 units were built in [Quebec](/wiki/Quebec "Quebec") and [New Brunswick](/wiki/New_Brunswick "New Brunswick"), as well as Pakistan, Argentina, South Korea, Romania, and China. A single example of a non-CANDU 6 design was sold to India. The multi-unit design was used only in [Ontario](/wiki/Ontario "Ontario"), Canada, and grew in size and power as more units were installed in the province, reaching ~880 MWe in the units installed at the [Darlington Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station"). An effort to optimize the larger units in a fashion similar to CANDU 6 led to the **CANDU 9**. 

By the early 2000s, sales prospects for the original CANDU designs were dwindling due to the introduction of newer designs from other companies. AECL responded by cancelling CANDU 9 development and moving to the [Advanced CANDU reactor](/wiki/Advanced_CANDU_reactor "Advanced CANDU reactor") (ACR) design. ACR failed to find any buyers; its last potential sale was for an expansion at Darlington, but this was cancelled in 2009. In October 2011, the Canadian Federal Government licensed the CANDU design to [Candu Energy](/wiki/Candu_Energy "Candu Energy") (a wholly owned subsidiary of SNC-Lavalin, now the [AtkinsRéalis Group Inc.](/wiki/AtkinsR%C3%A9alis "AtkinsRéalis")), which also acquired the former reactor development and marketing division of AECL at that time. 

In 2017, a consultation with industry led [Natural Resources Canada](/wiki/Natural_Resources_Canada "Natural Resources Canada") to establish a "SMR Roadmap"[](#cite_note-2) targeting the development of [small modular reactors](/wiki/Small_modular_reactor "Small modular reactor") (SMRs). In response, SNC-Lavalin developed a 300 MWe SMR version of the CANDU, the **CANDU SMR** , which it began to highlight on its website.[](#cite_note-3) In 2020, the CANDU SMR was not selected for further design work for a Canadian demonstration project. SNC-Lavalin is still looking at marketing a 300 MW SMR in part due to projected demand due to [climate change mitigation](/wiki/Climate_change_mitigation "Climate change mitigation").[](#cite_note-4)

The basic operation of the CANDU design is similar to other nuclear reactors. [Fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") reactions in the reactor core heat pressurized water in a _primary cooling loop_. A [heat exchanger](/wiki/Heat_exchanger "Heat exchanger"), also known as a [steam generator](/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\) "Steam generator \(nuclear power\)"), transfers the heat to a secondary cooling loop, which powers a steam [turbine](/wiki/Turbine "Turbine") with an [electric generator](/wiki/Electric_generator "Electric generator") attached to it (for a typical [Rankine thermodynamic cycle](/wiki/Rankine_cycle "Rankine cycle")). The exhaust steam from the turbines is then cooled, condensed and returned as feedwater to the steam generator. The final cooling often uses cooling water from a nearby source, such as a lake, river, or ocean. Newer CANDU plants, such as the [Darlington Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station") near [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto "Toronto"), Ontario, use a diffuser to spread the warm outlet water over a larger volume and limit the effects on the environment. Although all CANDU plants to date have used open-cycle cooling, modern CANDU designs can use [cooling towers](/wiki/Cooling_tower "Cooling tower") instead.[](#cite_note-5)

Where the CANDU design differs from most other designs is in the details of the fissile core and the primary cooling loop. [Natural uranium](/wiki/Natural_uranium "Natural uranium") consists of a mix of mostly [uranium-238](/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238") with small amounts of [uranium-235](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") and trace amounts of other isotopes. Fission in these elements releases high-energy [neutrons](/wiki/Neutron "Neutron"), which can cause other 235U atoms in the fuel to undergo fission as well. This process is more effective when the neutron energies are lower than what the reactions release naturally. Most reactors use some form of [neutron moderator](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") to lower the energy of the neutrons, or "[thermalize](/wiki/Thermal_neutron "Thermal neutron")" them, which makes the reaction more efficient. The energy lost by the neutrons during this moderation process heats the moderator, and this heat is extracted for power. 

Most commercial reactor designs use normal water as the moderator. Water absorbs some of the neutrons, enough that it is not possible to keep the reaction going in natural uranium. CANDU replaces this "light" water with [heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water"). Heavy water's extra neutron decreases its ability to absorb excess neutrons, resulting in a better [neutron economy](/wiki/Neutron_economy "Neutron economy"). This allows CANDU to run on unenriched [natural uranium](/wiki/Natural_uranium "Natural uranium"), or uranium mixed with a wide variety of other materials such as [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium "Plutonium") and [thorium](/wiki/Thorium "Thorium"). This was a major goal of the CANDU design; by operating on natural uranium the cost of enrichment is removed. This also presents an advantage in [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") terms, as there is no need for enrichment facilities, which might also be used for weapons. 

### Calandria and fuel design

[](/wiki/File:CANDU_fuel_bundles.jpg)Two CANDU fuel bundles: each is about 50 cm in length and 10 cm in diameter, and can generate about 1 [GWh](/wiki/Kilowatt-hour "Kilowatt-hour") (3.6 TJ) of electricity during its time in a CANDU reactor

In conventional [light-water reactor](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor") (LWR) designs, the entire fissile core is placed in a large [pressure vessel](/wiki/Pressure_vessel "Pressure vessel"). The amount of heat that can be removed by a unit of a coolant is a function of the temperature; by pressurizing the core, the water can be heated to much greater temperatures [before boiling](/wiki/Boiling_point "Boiling point"), thereby removing more heat and allowing the core to be smaller and more efficient. 

Building a pressure vessel of the required size is a significant challenge, and at the time of the CANDU's design, Canada's heavy industry lacked the requisite experience and capability to cast and machine reactor pressure vessels of the required size. This problem is amplified by natural uranium fuel's lower fissile density, which requires a larger reactor core. This issue was so major that even the relatively small pressure vessel originally intended for use in the [NPD](/wiki/Nuclear_Power_Demonstration "Nuclear Power Demonstration") prior to its mid-construction redesign could not be fabricated domestically and had to be manufactured in Scotland instead. Domestic development of the technology required to produce pressure vessels of the size required for commercial-scale heavy water moderated power reactors was thought to be very unlikely.[](#cite_note-Canada_Enters_the_Nuclear_Age-6)

In CANDU the fuel bundles of about 10 cm diameter are composed of many smaller metal tubes. The bundles are contained in pressure tubes within a larger vessel containing additional heavy water acting as a moderator. This larger vessel, known as a calandria, is not pressurized and remains at lower temperatures, making it easier to fabricate. In order to prevent the heat from the pressure tubes from leaking into the surrounding moderator, each pressure tube is enclosed in a calandria tube. [Carbon dioxide](/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") gas in the gap between the two tubes acts as an insulator. The moderator tank also acts as a large [heat sink](/wiki/Heat_sink "Heat sink") that provides an additional [safety](/wiki/Nuclear_safety "Nuclear safety") feature. 

In a conventional [pressurized water reactor](/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor "Pressurized water reactor"), refuelling the system requires shutting down the core and opening the pressure vessel. In CANDU reactors, the tube being refuelled remains pressurized. This allows the CANDU system to be continually refuelled without shutting down, another major design goal. In modern systems, two robotic machines attach to the reactor faces and open the end caps of a pressure tube. One machine pushes in the new fuel, whereby the depleted fuel is pushed out and collected at the other end. A significant operational advantage of online refuelling is that a failed or leaking fuel bundle can be removed from the core once it has been located, thus reducing the radiation levels in the primary cooling loop. 

Each fuel bundle is a cylinder assembled from thin tubes filled with ceramic pellets of uranium oxide fuel (fuel elements). In older designs, the bundle had 28 or 37 half-meter-long fuel elements with 12–13 such assemblies lying end-to-end in a pressure tube. The newer [CANFLEX](/wiki/CANFLEX "CANFLEX") bundle has 43 fuel elements, with two element sizes (so the power rating can be increased without melting the hottest fuel elements). It is about 10 centimetres (3.9 in) in diameter, 50 centimetres (20 in) long, weighs about 20 kilograms (44 lb), and is intended to eventually replace the 37-element bundle. To allow the [neutrons](/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") to flow freely between the bundles, the tubes and bundles are made of neutron-transparent [zircaloy](/wiki/Zirconium_alloy "Zirconium alloy") ([zirconium](/wiki/Zirconium "Zirconium") \+ 2.5% wt [niobium](/wiki/Niobium "Niobium")). 

### Purpose of using heavy water

Further information: [nuclear reactor physics](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor_physics "Nuclear reactor physics"), [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission"), and [heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water")

[](/wiki/File:Bruce-Nuclear-Szmurlo.jpg)[Bruce Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Bruce_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Bruce Nuclear Generating Station"), operating eight CANDU reactors

Natural uranium is a mix of [isotopes](/wiki/Isotope "Isotope"): approximately 99.28% [uranium-238](/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238") and 0.72% [uranium-235](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") by atom fraction. Nuclear power reactors are usually operated at constant power for long periods of time, which requires a constant rate of fission over time. In order to keep the fission rate constant, the neutrons released by fission must produce an equal number of fissions in other fuel atoms. This balance is referred to as [criticality](/wiki/Critical_mass "Critical mass"). Neutrons released by nuclear fission are fairly energetic and are not readily absorbed (or "captured") by the surrounding [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material"). In order to improve the capture rate, the neutron energy must be reduced, or _moderated_ , to be as low as possible. In practice, the lower energy limit is the energy where the neutrons are in thermal equilibrium with the moderator. When neutrons approach this lower energy limit, they are referred to as [thermal neutrons](/wiki/Thermal_neutron "Thermal neutron"). 

During moderation it helps to separate the neutrons and uranium, since 238U has a large affinity for intermediate-energy neutrons ("resonance" absorption), but is only easily fissioned by the few energetic neutrons above ≈1.5–2 [MeV](/wiki/MeV "MeV"). Since most of the fuel material is usually 238U, most reactor designs are based on thin fuel rods separated by moderator, allowing the neutrons to travel in the moderator before entering the fuel again. More neutrons are released than the minimum needed to maintain the chain reaction; when uranium-238 absorbs neutrons, plutonium is created, which helps to make up for the depletion of uranium-235. Eventually the build-up of [fission products](/wiki/Fission_product "Fission product") that are more neutron-absorbing than 238U slows the reaction and calls for refuelling. 

Light water makes an excellent moderator: the [light hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") atoms are very close in mass to a neutron and can absorb a lot of energy in a single collision (like a collision of two billiard balls). However, light hydrogen can absorb neutrons, reducing the number available to react with the small amount of 235U in natural uranium, preventing criticality. In order to allow criticality, the fuel must be [enriched](/wiki/Enriched_uranium "Enriched uranium"), increasing the amount of 235U to a usable level. In [light-water reactors](/wiki/Light-water_reactor "Light-water reactor"), the fuel is typically enriched to between 2% and 5% 235U (the leftover fraction with less 235U is called [depleted uranium](/wiki/Depleted_uranium "Depleted uranium")). Enrichment facilities are expensive to build and operate. They may also pose a [proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation") concern, as they can be used to enrich the 235U much further, up to [weapons-grade](/wiki/Weapons-grade "Weapons-grade") material (90% or more 235U). This can be remedied if the fuel is supplied and reprocessed by an [internationally approved](/wiki/International_Atomic_Energy_Agency "International Atomic Energy Agency") supplier. 

The main advantage of [heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water") [moderator](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") over light water is the reduced absorption of the neutrons that sustain the chain reaction, allowing a lower concentration of fissile atoms (to the point of using unenriched natural uranium fuel). [Deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium") ("heavy hydrogen") already has the extra neutron that light hydrogen would absorb, reducing the tendency to capture neutrons. Deuterium has twice the mass of a single neutron (vs light hydrogen, which has about the same mass); the mismatch means that more collisions are needed to moderate the neutrons, requiring a larger thickness of moderator between the fuel rods. This increases the size of the reactor core and the leakage of neutrons. It is also the practical reason for the calandria design, otherwise, a very large pressure vessel would be needed.[](#cite_note-Basic_CANDU_Design-7) The low 235U density in natural uranium also implies that less of the fuel will be consumed before the fission rate drops too low to sustain criticality, because the ratio of 235U to fission products + 238U is lower. In CANDU most of the moderator is at lower temperatures than in other designs, reducing the spread of speeds and the overall speed of the moderator particles. This means that most of the neutrons will end up at a lower energy and be more likely to cause fission, so CANDU not only "burns" natural uranium, but it does so more effectively as well. Overall, CANDU reactors use 30–40% less mined uranium than light-water reactors per unit of electricity produced. This is a major advantage of the heavy-water design; it not only requires less fuel, but as the fuel does not have to be enriched, it is much less expensive as well. 

A further unique feature of heavy-water moderation is the greater stability of the [chain reaction](/wiki/Chain_reaction "Chain reaction"). This is due to the relatively low binding energy of the deuterium nucleus (2.2 MeV), leading to some [energetic neutrons](/wiki/N,2n "N,2n") and especially [gamma rays](/wiki/Photodisintegration "Photodisintegration") breaking the deuterium nuclei apart to produce extra neutrons. Both gammas produced directly by fission and by the decay of [fission fragments](/wiki/Nuclear_fission_products "Nuclear fission products") have enough energy, and the half-lives of the fission fragments range from seconds to hours or even years. The slow response of these gamma-generated neutrons delays the [response of the reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction#Timescales_of_nuclear_chain_reactions "Nuclear chain reaction") and gives the operators extra time in case of an emergency. Since gamma rays travel for meters through water, an increased rate of chain reaction in one part of the reactor will produce a response from the rest of the reactor, allowing various negative feedbacks to stabilize the reaction. 

On the other hand, the fission neutrons are thoroughly slowed down before they reach another fuel rod, meaning that it takes neutrons a longer time to get from one part of the reactor to the other. Thus if the chain reaction accelerates in one section of the reactor, the change will propagate itself only slowly to the rest of the core, giving time to respond in an emergency. The independence of the neutrons' energies from the nuclear fuel used is what allows such fuel flexibility in a CANDU reactor, since every fuel bundle will experience the same environment and affect its neighbors in the same way, whether the fissile material is uranium-235, [uranium-233](/wiki/Uranium-233 "Uranium-233") or [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium "Plutonium"). 

Canada developed the heavy-water-moderated design in the post–[World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") era to explore nuclear energy while lacking access to enrichment facilities. War-era enrichment systems were extremely expensive to build and operate, whereas the heavy water solution allowed the use of natural uranium in the experimental [ZEEP](/wiki/ZEEP "ZEEP") reactor. A much less expensive enrichment system was developed, but the United States classified work on the [cheaper gas centrifuge](/wiki/Zippe-type_centrifuge#Centrifuge_uranium_enrichment "Zippe-type centrifuge") process. The CANDU was therefore designed to use natural uranium. 

## Safety features

The CANDU includes several active and passive safety features in its design. Some of these are a side effect of the physical layout of the system. 

CANDU designs have a positive [void coefficient](/wiki/Void_coefficient "Void coefficient"), as well as a small power coefficient, normally considered bad in reactor design. This implies that steam generated in the coolant will increase the reaction rate, which in turn would generate more steam. This is one of the many reasons for the cooler mass of moderator in the calandria, as even a serious steam incident in the core would not have a major impact on the overall moderation cycle. Only if the moderator itself starts to boil would there be any significant effect, and the large thermal mass ensures that this will occur slowly. The deliberately "sluggish" response of the fission process in CANDU allows controllers more time to diagnose and deal with problems.[](#cite_note-safe-8)

The fuel channels can only maintain criticality if they are mechanically sound. If the temperature of the fuel bundles increases to the point where they are mechanically unstable, their horizontal layout means that they will bend under gravity, shifting the layout of the bundles and reducing the efficiency of the reactions. Because the original fuel arrangement is optimal for a chain reaction, and the natural uranium fuel has little excess reactivity, any significant deformation will stop the inter-fuel pellet fission reaction. This will not stop heat production from fission product decay, which would continue to supply a considerable heat output. If this process further weakens the fuel bundles, the pressure tube they are in will eventually bend far enough to touch the calandria tube, allowing heat to be transferred into the moderator tank. The moderator vessel has a considerable thermal capability on its own and is normally kept relatively cool.[](#cite_note-safe-8)

Heat generated by fission products would initially be at about 7% of full reactor power, which requires significant cooling. The CANDU designs have several emergency cooling systems, as well as having limited self-pumping capability through thermal means (the steam generator is well above the reactor). Even in the event of a catastrophic accident and core [meltdown](/wiki/Nuclear_meltdown "Nuclear meltdown"), the fuel is not critical in light water.[](#cite_note-safe-8) This means that cooling the core with water from nearby sources will not add to the reactivity of the fuel mass. 

Normally the rate of fission is controlled by light-water compartments called liquid zone controllers, which absorb excess neutrons, and by adjuster rods, which can be raised or lowered in the core to control the neutron flux. These are used for normal operation, allowing the controllers to adjust reactivity across the fuel mass, as different portions would normally burn at different rates depending on their position. The adjuster rods can also be used to slow or stop criticality. Because these rods are inserted into the low-pressure calandria, not the high-pressure fuel tubes, they would not be "ejected" by steam, a design issue for many pressurized-water reactors. 

There are two independent, fast-acting safety shutdown systems as well. Shutoff rods are held above the reactor by electromagnets and drop under gravity into the core to quickly end criticality. This system works even in the event of a complete power failure, as the electromagnets only hold the rods out of the reactor when power is available. A secondary system injects a high-pressure [gadolinium nitrate](/wiki/Gadolinium_nitrate "Gadolinium nitrate") neutron absorber solution into the calandria.[](#cite_note-9)

## Fuel cycle

[](/wiki/File:CANDU_fuel_cycles.jpg)Range of possible CANDU fuel cycles: CANDU reactors can accept a variety of fuel types, including the used fuel from light-water reactors.

A heavy-water design can sustain a chain reaction with a lower concentration of fissile atoms than light-water reactors, allowing it to use some alternative fuels; for example, "[recovered uranium](/wiki/Reprocessed_uranium "Reprocessed uranium")" (RU) from used LWR fuel. CANDU was designed for natural uranium with only 0.7% 235U, so reprocessed uranium with 0.9% 235U is a comparatively rich fuel. This extracts a further 30–40% energy from the uranium. The Qinshan CANDU reactor in China has used recovered uranium.[](#cite_note-10) The DUPIC (_Direct Use of spent PWR fuel in CANDU_) process under development can recycle it even without reprocessing. The fuel is sintered in air (oxidized), then in hydrogen (reduced) to break it into a powder, which is then formed into CANDU fuel pellets. 

CANDU reactors can also [breed fuel](/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle "Thorium fuel cycle") from the more abundant [thorium](/wiki/Thorium "Thorium"). This is being [investigated](/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle "Thorium fuel cycle") by India to take advantage of its natural thorium reserves.[](#cite_note-:0-1)

CANDU reactors may also utilize a mix of uranium and plutonium oxides ([MOX fuel](/wiki/MOX_fuel "MOX fuel")), the plutonium either from dismantled [nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon") or reprocessed reactor fuel. The mix of isotopes in reprocessed plutonium is not attractive for weapons, but can be used as fuel (instead of being simply nuclear waste), while consuming [weapons-grade plutonium](/wiki/Weapons-grade_plutonium "Weapons-grade plutonium") eliminates a proliferation hazard. If the aim is explicitly to utilize plutonium or other [actinides](/wiki/Actinide "Actinide") from spent fuel, then special inert-matrix fuels are proposed to do this more efficiently than MOX. Since they contain no uranium, these fuels do not breed any extra plutonium. 

## Economics

The neutron economy of heavy-water moderation and precise control of on-line refueling allow CANDU to use a wide range of fuels other than enriched uranium, e.g., natural uranium, reprocessed uranium, [thorium](/wiki/Thorium "Thorium"), [plutonium](/wiki/Plutonium "Plutonium"), and used LWR fuel. Given the expense of enrichment, this can make fuel much cheaper. There is an initial investment into the tonnes of 99.75% pure[](#cite_note-11) heavy water to fill the core and heat-transfer system. In the case of the Darlington plant, costs released as part of a [freedom of information act](/wiki/Freedom_of_information_act "Freedom of information act") request put the [overnight cost](/wiki/Overnight_cost "Overnight cost") of the plant (four reactors totalling 3,512 MWe net capacity) at $5.117 billion CAD (about US$4.2 billion at early-1990s exchange rates). Total capital costs including interest were $14.319 billion CAD (about US$11.9 billion) with the heavy water accounting for $1.528 billion, or 11%, of this.[](#cite_note-12)

Since heavy water is less efficient than light water at slowing neutrons,[](#cite_note-13) CANDU needs a larger moderator-to-fuel ratio and a larger core for the same power output. Although a calandria-based core is cheaper to build, its size increases the cost for standard features like the [containment building](/wiki/Containment_building "Containment building"). Generally nuclear plant construction and operations are ≈65% of overall lifetime cost; for CANDU, costs are dominated by construction even more. Fueling CANDU is cheaper than other reactors, costing only ≈10% of the total, so the overall price per kWh electricity is comparable. 

When first introduced, CANDUs offered much better [capacity factor](/wiki/Capacity_factor "Capacity factor") (ratio of power generated to what would be generated by running at full power, 100% of the time) than LWRs of a similar generation. The light-water designs spent, on average, about half the time being refueled or maintained. Since the 1980s, dramatic improvements in LWR outage management[_[which?](/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words "Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words")_] have narrowed the gap, with several units achieving capacity factors ~90% and higher, with an overall US fleet performance of 92% in 2010.[](#cite_note-14) The latest-generation CANDU 6 reactors have an 88–90% CF, but overall performance is dominated by the older Canadian units with CFs on the order of 80%.[](#cite_note-15) Refurbished units had historically demonstrated poor performance, on the order of 65%.[](#cite_note-16) This has since improved with the return of Bruce A units 1 and 2 to operation, which have post-refurbishment (2013+) capacity factors of 90.78% and 90.38%, respectively.[](#cite_note-pris_iaea_org-17)

Some CANDU plants suffered from [cost overruns](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station#Cost_overruns "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station") during construction, often from external factors such as government action.[](#cite_note-18) For instance, imposed construction delays led to roughly a doubling of the cost of the Darlington Nuclear Generating Station near Toronto, Ontario. Technical problems and redesigns added about another billion to the resulting $14.4 billion price.[](#cite_note-19) In 2002 two CANDU 6 reactors at Qinshan in China were completed on-schedule and on-budget, an achievement attributed to tight control over scope and schedule.[](#cite_note-20)

[](/wiki/File:Pickering-nuclear-generating-station-001.jpg) [Pickering Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Pickering_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Pickering Nuclear Generating Station") The station consists of four operating and four shut down CANDU reactors housed in domed containment buildings. The cylindrical Vacuum Building is an additional safety system where steam is condensed in the event of a major leak.

## Tritium production

[Tritium](/wiki/Tritium "Tritium") is a radioactive isotope of [hydrogen](/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") with a [half-life](/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") of 12.3 years. Due to this short half-life, it does not persist in nature. It is continuously produced in small amounts, about 4 kg per year globally, by [cosmic ray](/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray") interactions in the upper atmosphere. This is not recovered for industrial or research use. 

Tritium is also generated in the fuel of all nuclear fission reactors. In CANDU reactors, additional tritium is generated in the [heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water") coolant and moderator due to [neutron capture](/wiki/Neutron_capture "Neutron capture") in [deuterium](/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium"). Heavy water is relatively immune to neutron capture, but a small amount of the deuterium turns into [tritium](/wiki/Tritium "Tritium") in this way. A CANDU reactor's production of tritium is therefore greater per unit of power production than other widely deployed reactor types in commercial power operation. 

This tritium is extracted from some CANDU plants in Canada, mainly to improve safety in case of heavy-water leakage. The gas is stored and used in a variety of commercial products, notably ["powerless" lighting systems](/wiki/Tritium_illumination "Tritium illumination") and medical devices. It also has use in research. 

### Nuclear fusion

[Fusion power](/wiki/Fusion_power "Fusion power") is a developmental energy technology that operates on different principles to nuclear fission reactors like CANDU. However, the most actively explored fuel for possible future fusion power plants is [deuterium and tritium](/wiki/Deuterium%E2%80%93tritium_fusion "Deuterium–tritium fusion"). Tritium is much scarcer than deuterium, which is a component of [seawater](/wiki/Seawater "Seawater"), making tritium supply a key limit on fusion research and deployment. Nuclear fission reactors are currently the main source of tritium, and in ordinary commercial power operation CANDU reactors specifically are the largest producers. The CANDU fleet is therefore the main source of tritium for fusion research and is expected to be the main source of start-up tritium for possible future fusion power plants.[](#cite_note-clery_gas-21)

Future demands for tritium appear to outstrip production, in particular the demands of future generations of experimental [fusion reactors](/wiki/Fusion_reactor "Fusion reactor") like [ITER](/wiki/ITER "ITER"), with up to 10kg of tritium being required in order to start up a fusion reactor and so dozens of kilograms being required for a fleet.[](#cite_note-clery_gas-21) Between 1.5 to 2.1 kilograms (3.3 to 4.6 lb) of tritium were recovered annually at the Darlington separation facility in Canada by 2003, of which a minor fraction was sold.[](#cite_note-22): 10 Consequently, the [Canadian Nuclear Laboratories](/wiki/Canadian_Nuclear_Laboratories_Research_Facilities "Canadian Nuclear Laboratories Research Facilities") in 2024 announced a decades-long program to refurbish existing CANDU plants and equip them with tritium breeding facilities.[](#cite_note-23)

### Safety

Tritium is considered a weak [radionuclide](/wiki/Radionuclide "Radionuclide") because of its low-energy radioactive emissions ([beta particle](/wiki/Beta_particle "Beta particle") energy up to 18.6 keV).[](#cite_note-tritium-24) The beta particles travel 6 mm in air and only penetrate skin up to 6 micrometers. If inhaled or ingested, however, the [elimination half-life](/wiki/Elimination_half-life "Elimination half-life") of tritium is 10–12 days,[](#cite_note-tritium-24) in which time it is a serious danger to human health. 

With production on the scale of 1 kg per year, tritium is a very small proportion of CANDU nuclear waste by both weight and radioactivity. Some of the tritium produced by CANDU reactors escapes into containment. Most is recovered, but about 1% escapes containment. Although higher than in other reactor types that produce less tritium, this is considered a routine radioactive emission. Tritium levels in the environment around a plant are monitored. Typical emissions from CANDU plants in Canada are less than 1% of the national regulatory limit, based on [ICRP](/wiki/International_Commission_on_Radiological_Protection "International Commission on Radiological Protection") guidelines.[](#cite_note-25)[](#cite_note-26)[](#cite_note-green-27)[](#cite_note-28)

Tritium emission by CANDU plants has been a cause of civil society concern. In 2007 [Greenpeace](/wiki/Greenpeace "Greenpeace") published a critique of tritium emissions from Canadian nuclear power plants by [Ian Fairlie](/wiki/Ian_Fairlie "Ian Fairlie").[](#cite_note-green-27) This report was criticized[](#cite_note-29) by Canadian Radiation Protection Association founding president Richard Osborne.[](#cite_note-30)[](#cite_note-31)

## Nuclear nonproliferation

Main article: [Nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation")

CANDU reactors have features that may make them relatively attractive as sources for plutonium for nuclear weapons and raise concerns about their potential contribution to [nuclear proliferation](/wiki/Nuclear_proliferation "Nuclear proliferation"). In normal operations, the isotopic composition of plutonium contained in spent fuel is far from [weapons-grade](/wiki/Weapons-grade_nuclear_material "Weapons-grade nuclear material"), but the ability to load and unload fuel while the reactor is operating allows for production of very low burnup spent fuel containing weapon-grade plutonium.[](#cite_note-32) However, the fuel handling machines for CANDU reactors are not designed to operate at high throughput and safeguards measures at CANDU reactors make such operations relatively easy to detect.[](#cite_note-33)[](#cite_note-34)

The plutonium for India's first nuclear detonation, [Operation Smiling Buddha](/wiki/Operation_Smiling_Buddha "Operation Smiling Buddha") in 1974, was produced in a [CIRUS reactor](/wiki/CIRUS_reactor "CIRUS reactor") supplied by Canada and partially paid for by the Canadian government using heavy water supplied by the United States.[](#cite_note-35) In addition to its two PHWR reactors, India has some safeguarded [pressurised heavy-water reactors](/wiki/Pressurised_heavy-water_reactor "Pressurised heavy-water reactor") (PHWRs) based on the CANDU design, and two safeguarded light-water reactors supplied by the US. Plutonium has been extracted from the spent fuel from all of these reactors;[](#cite_note-36) India mainly relies on an Indian designed and built military reactor called [Dhruva](/wiki/Dhruva_reactor "Dhruva reactor"). The design is believed to be derived from the CIRUS reactor, with the Dhruva being scaled-up for more efficient plutonium production. It is this reactor which is thought to have produced the plutonium for India's more recent (1998) [Operation Shakti](/wiki/Operation_Shakti "Operation Shakti") nuclear tests.[](#cite_note-37)

The 1998 Operation Shakti test series in India included one bomb of about 45 kilotons of TNT (190 TJ) yield that India has publicly claimed was a hydrogen bomb. An offhand comment in the [BARC](/wiki/Bhabha_Atomic_Research_Centre "Bhabha Atomic Research Centre") publication _Heavy Water – Properties, Production and Analysis_ appears to suggest that the tritium was extracted from the heavy water in the CANDU and PHWR reactors in commercial operation. _Janes Intelligence Review_ quotes the Chairman of the Indian Atomic Energy Commission as admitting to the tritium extraction plant, but refusing to comment on its use.[](#cite_note-38) India is also capable of creating tritium more efficiently by irradiation of lithium-6 in reactors. 

In 1985 what was then Ontario Hydro sparked controversy in Ontario due to its proposed sale of tritium to the United States. The plan, by law, involved sales for non-military applications only, but some speculated that the exports could have freed American tritium for the United States nuclear weapons program. Ontario Hydro began extracting tritium from CANDU in October 1988 and by the end of the [Cold War](/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War") Canada had not actually exported any CANDU-derived tritium.[](#cite_note-39)

## History

The CANDU development effort has gone through four major stages over time. The first systems were experimental and prototype machines of limited power. These were replaced by a second generation of machines of 500 to 600 MWe (the CANDU 6), a series of larger machines of 900 MWe, and finally developing into the CANDU 9 and ACR-1000 effort.[](#cite_note-slides-40)[](#cite_note-CANDU_Evolution-41)

### Early efforts

The first heavy-water-moderated design in Canada was the [ZEEP](/wiki/ZEEP "ZEEP"), which started operation just after the end of [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). ZEEP was joined by several other experimental machines, including the [NRX](/wiki/NRX "NRX") in 1947 and [NRU](/wiki/National_Research_Universal_reactor "National Research Universal reactor") in 1957. These efforts led to the first CANDU-type reactor, the [Nuclear Power Demonstration](/wiki/Nuclear_Power_Demonstration "Nuclear Power Demonstration") (NPD), in Rolphton, Ontario. It was intended as a proof-of-concept and rated for only 22 [MWe](/wiki/MWe "MWe"), a very low power for a commercial power reactor. NPD produced the first nuclear-generated electricity in Canada and ran successfully from 1962 to 1987.[](#cite_note-42)[](#cite_note-43)

The second CANDU was the [Douglas Point](/wiki/Douglas_Point "Douglas Point") reactor, a more powerful version rated at roughly 200 MWe and located near [Kincardine](/wiki/Kincardine,_Ontario "Kincardine, Ontario"), Ontario. It went into service in 1968 and ran until 1984. Uniquely among CANDU stations, Douglas Point had an oil-filled window with a view of the east reactor face, even when the reactor was operating. Douglas Point was originally planned to be a two-unit station, but the second unit was cancelled because of the success of the larger 515 MWe units at [Pickering](/wiki/Pickering_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Pickering Nuclear Generating Station").[](#cite_note-44)[](#cite_note-45)

[](/wiki/File:Centrale_nucl%C3%A9aire_Gentilly.jpg) Gentilly-1 (right) and Gentilly-2 (left)

[Gentilly-1](/wiki/Gentilly_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Gentilly Nuclear Generating Station"), in [Bécancour, Quebec](/wiki/B%C3%A9cancour,_Quebec "Bécancour, Quebec"), near [Trois-Rivières](/wiki/Trois-Rivi%C3%A8res "Trois-Rivières"), Quebec, was also an experimental version of CANDU, using a boiling light-water coolant and vertical pressure tubes, but was not considered successful and closed after seven years of fitful operation.[](#cite_note-46) Gentilly-2, a CANDU-6 reactor, began operating in 1983. Following statements from the in-coming [Parti Québécois](/wiki/Parti_Qu%C3%A9b%C3%A9cois "Parti Québécois") government in September 2012 that Gentilly would close, the operator, [Hydro-Québec](/wiki/Hydro-Qu%C3%A9bec "Hydro-Québec"), decided to cancel a previously announced refurbishment of the plant and announced its shutdown at the end of 2012, citing economic reasons for the decision. The company has started a 50-year [decommissioning](/wiki/Nuclear_decommissioning "Nuclear decommissioning") process estimated to cost $1.8 billion.[](#cite_note-47)

In parallel with the classic CANDU design, experimental variants were being developed. [WR-1](/wiki/WR-1 "WR-1"), located at the [AECL](/wiki/Atomic_Energy_of_Canada_Limited "Atomic Energy of Canada Limited")'s [Whiteshell Laboratories](/wiki/Whiteshell_Laboratories "Whiteshell Laboratories") in [Pinawa, Manitoba](/wiki/Pinawa,_Manitoba "Pinawa, Manitoba"), used vertical pressure tubes and organic [oil](/wiki/Petroleum "Petroleum") as the primary coolant. The oil used has a higher boiling point than water, allowing the reactor to operate at higher temperatures and lower pressures than a conventional reactor. WR-1's outlet temperature was about 490 °C compared to the CANDU 6's nominal 310 °C; the higher temperature and thus thermodynamic efficiency offsets to some degree the fact that oils have about half the heat capacity of water. The higher temperatures also result in more efficient conversion to steam, and ultimately, electricity. WR-1 operated successfully for many years and promised a significantly higher efficiency than water-cooled versions.[](#cite_note-48)[](#cite_note-49)

### 600 MWe designs

The successes at NPD and Douglas Point led to the decision to construct the first multi-unit station in Pickering, Ontario. Pickering A, consisting of Units 1 to 4, went into service in 1971. Pickering B with units 5 to 8 came online in 1983, giving a full-station capacity of 4,120 MWe. The station is very close to the city of [Toronto](/wiki/Toronto "Toronto"), in order to reduce [transmission](/wiki/Electric_power_transmission "Electric power transmission") costs. 

A series of improvements to the basic Pickering design led to the CANDU 6 design, which first went into operation in the early 1980s. CANDU 6 was essentially a version of the Pickering power plant that was redesigned to be able to be built in single-reactor units. CANDU 6 was used in several installations outside Ontario, including the [Gentilly-2](/wiki/Gentilly_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Gentilly Nuclear Generating Station") in Quebec, and [Point Lepreau Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Point_Lepreau_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Point Lepreau Nuclear Generating Station") in New Brunswick. CANDU 6 forms the majority of foreign CANDU systems, including the designs exported to Argentina, Romania, China and South Korea. Only India operates a CANDU system that is not based on the CANDU 6 design. 

### 900 MWe designs

The [economics of nuclear power plants](/wiki/Economics_of_nuclear_power_plants "Economics of nuclear power plants") generally scale well with size. This improvement at larger sizes is offset by the sudden appearance of large quantities of power on the grid, which leads to a lowering of electricity prices through supply and demand effects. Predictions in the late 1960s suggested that growth in electricity demand would overwhelm these downward pricing pressures, leading most designers to introduce plants in the 1000 MWe range. 

Pickering A was quickly followed by such an upscaling effort for the [Bruce Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Bruce_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Bruce Nuclear Generating Station"), constructed in stages between 1970 and 1987. It is the largest nuclear facility in North America and second largest in the world (after [Kashiwazaki-Kariwa](/wiki/Kashiwazaki-Kariwa_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Kashiwazaki-Kariwa Nuclear Power Plant") in Japan), with eight [reactors](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") at around 800 MWe each, in total 6,232 MW (net) and 7,276 MW (gross). Another, smaller, upscaling led to the [Darlington Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station") design, similar to the Bruce plant, but delivering about 880 MWe per reactor in a four-reactor station. 

As was the case for the development of the Pickering design into the CANDU 6, the Bruce design was also developed into the similar CANDU 9.[](#cite_note-50) Like the CANDU 6, the CANDU 9 is essentially a repackaging of the Bruce design, so that it can be built as a single-reactor unit. No CANDU 9 reactors have been built. 

### Generation III+ designs

Main article: [Advanced CANDU reactor](/wiki/Advanced_CANDU_reactor "Advanced CANDU reactor")

Through the 1980s and 1990s the nuclear power market suffered a major crash, with few new plants being constructed in North America or Europe. Design work continued throughout, and new design concepts were introduced that dramatically improved safety, capital costs, economics and overall performance. These [generation III+](/wiki/Generation_III_reactor "Generation III reactor") and [generation IV](/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor "Generation IV reactor") machines became a topic of considerable interest in the early 2000s, as it appeared that a [nuclear renaissance](/wiki/Nuclear_renaissance "Nuclear renaissance") was underway and large numbers of new reactors would be built over the next decade.[](#cite_note-51)

AECL had been working on a design known as the ACR-700, using elements of the latest versions of the CANDU 6 and CANDU 9, with a design power of 700 MWe.[](#cite_note-CANDU_Evolution-41) During the nuclear renaissance, the upscaling seen in the earlier years re-expressed itself, and the ACR-700 was developed into the 1200 MWe ACR-1000. ACR-1000 is the next-generation (officially, "generation III+") CANDU technology, which makes some significant modifications to the existing CANDU design.[](#cite_note-acr1000-52)

The main change, and the most radical among the CANDU generations, is the use of pressurized light water as the coolant. This significantly reduces the cost of implementing the primary cooling loop, which no longer has to be filled with expensive heavy water. The ACR-1000 uses about 1/3rd the heavy water needed in earlier-generation designs. It also eliminates tritium production in the coolant loop, the major source of tritium leaks in operational CANDU designs. The redesign also allows a slightly negative [void reactivity](/wiki/Void_coefficient "Void coefficient"), a major design goal of all Gen III+ machines.[](#cite_note-acr1000-52)

The design also requires the use of [slightly enriched uranium](/wiki/Enriched_uranium#slightly_enriched_uranium "Enriched uranium"), enriched by about 1 or 2%. The main reason for this is to increase the burn-up ratio, allowing bundles to remain in the reactor longer, so that only a third as much spent fuel is produced. This also has effects on operational costs and timetables, as the refuelling frequency is reduced. As is the case with earlier CANDU designs, the ACR-1000 also offers online refuelling.[](#cite_note-acr1000-52)

Outside of the reactor, the ACR-1000 has a number of design changes that are expected to dramatically lower capital and operational costs. Primary among these changes is the design lifetime of 60 years, which dramatically lowers the price of the electricity generated over the lifetime of the plant. The design also has an expected capacity factor of 90%. Higher-pressure steam generators and turbines improve efficiency downstream of the reactor.[](#cite_note-acr1000-52)

Many of the operational design changes were also applied to the existing CANDU 6 to produce the Enhanced CANDU 6\. Also known as CANDU 6e or EC 6, this was an evolutionary upgrade of the CANDU 6 design with a gross output of 740 MWe per unit. The reactors are designed with a lifetime of over 50 years, with a mid-life program to replace some of the key components e.g. the fuel channels. The projected average annual [capacity factor](/wiki/Capacity_factor "Capacity factor") is more than 90%. Improvements to construction techniques (including modular, open-top assembly) decrease construction costs. The CANDU 6e is designed to operate at power settings as low as 50%, allowing them to adjust to load demand much better than the previous designs.[](#cite_note-53)

### Sales efforts in Canada

By most measures, the CANDU is "the Ontario reactor". The system was developed almost entirely in Ontario, and only two experimental designs were built in other provinces. Of the 29 commercial CANDU reactors built, 22 are in Ontario. Of these 22, a number of reactors have been removed from service. Two new CANDU reactors have been proposed for Darlington with Canadian government help with financing,[](#cite_note-54) but these plans ended in 2009 due to high costs.[](#cite_note-55)

AECL has heavily marketed CANDU within Canada, but has found a limited reception. To date, only two non-experimental reactors have been built in other provinces, one each in Quebec and New Brunswick, other provinces have concentrated on hydro and coal-fired plants. Several Canadian provinces have developed large amounts of hydro power. Alberta and Saskatchewan do not have extensive hydro resources, and use mainly fossil fuels to generate electric power. 

Interest has been expressed in [Western Canada](/wiki/Western_Canada "Western Canada"), where CANDU reactors are being considered as heat and electricity sources for the energy-intensive [oil sands](/wiki/Tar_sands "Tar sands") extraction process, which currently uses [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas"). [Energy Alberta Corporation](/wiki/Energy_Alberta_Corporation "Energy Alberta Corporation") announced 27 August 2007 that they had applied for a licence to build a new nuclear plant at Lac Cardinal (30 km west of the town of [Peace River, Alberta](/wiki/Peace_River,_Alberta "Peace River, Alberta")), with two ACR-1000 reactors going online in 2017 producing 2.2 [gigawatts](/wiki/Gigawatt "Gigawatt") (electric).[](#cite_note-56) A 2007 parliamentary review suggested placing the development efforts on hold.[](#cite_note-57) The company was later purchased by Bruce Power,[](#cite_note-58) who proposed expanding the plant to four units of a total 4.4 gigawatts.[](#cite_note-59) These plans were upset and Bruce later withdrew its application for the Lac Cardinal, proposing instead a new site about 60 km away.[](#cite_note-60) The plans are currently moribund after a wide consultation with the public demonstrated that while about 1⁄5 of the population were open to reactors, 1⁄4 were opposed.[](#cite_note-61)[](#cite_note-62)

### Foreign sales

During the 1970s, the international nuclear sales market was extremely competitive, with many national nuclear companies being supported by their governments' foreign embassies. In addition, the pace of construction in the United States had meant that cost overruns and delayed completion was generally over, and subsequent reactors would be cheaper. Canada, a relatively new player on the international market, had numerous disadvantages in these efforts. The CANDU was deliberately designed to reduce the need for very large machined parts, making it suitable for construction by countries without a major industrial base. Sales efforts have had their most success in countries that could not locally build designs from other firms. 

In the late 1970s, AECL noted that each reactor sale would employ 3,600 Canadians and result in $300 million in balance-of-payments income.[](#cite_note-63) These sales efforts were aimed primarily at countries being run by dictatorships or similar, a fact that led to serious concerns in parliament.[](#cite_note-64) These efforts also led to a scandal when it was discovered millions of dollars had been given to foreign sales agents, with little or no record of who they were, or what they did to earn the money.[](#cite_note-65) This led to a [Royal Canadian Mounted Police](/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Mounted_Police "Royal Canadian Mounted Police") investigation after questions were raised about sales efforts in Argentina, and new regulations on full disclosure of fees for future sales.[](#cite_note-66)

CANDU's first success was the sale of early CANDU designs to India. In 1963, an agreement was signed for export of a 200 MWe power reactor based on the Douglas Point reactor. The success of the deal led to the 1966 sale of a second reactor of the same design. The first reactor, then known as [RAPP-1](/wiki/Rajasthan_Atomic_Power_Station "Rajasthan Atomic Power Station") for "Rajasthan Atomic Power Project", began operation in 1972. A serious problem with cracking of the reactor's end shield led to the reactor being shut down for long periods, and the reactor was finally downrated to 100 MW.[](#cite_note-67) Construction of the RAPP-2 reactor was still underway when India detonated its first [atomic bomb](/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb") in 1974, leading to Canada ending nuclear dealings with the country. Part of the sales agreement was a technology transfer process. When Canada withdrew from development, India continued construction of CANDU-like plants across the country.[](#cite_note-68) By 2010, CANDU-based reactors were operational at the following sites: Kaiga (3), Kakrapar (2), Madras (2), Narora (2), Rajasthan (6), and Tarapur (2). 

In Pakistan, the [Karachi Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Karachi_Nuclear_Power_Complex "Karachi Nuclear Power Complex") with a gross capacity of 137 MWe was built between 1966 and 1971. 

In 1972, AECL submitted a design based on the Pickering plant to Argentina's Comision Nacional de Energia Atomica process, in partnership with the Italian company Italimpianti. High inflation during construction led to massive losses, and efforts to re-negotiate the deal were interrupted by the March 1976 coup led by General Videla. The [Embalse Nuclear Power Station](/wiki/Embalse_Nuclear_Power_Station "Embalse Nuclear Power Station") began commercial operation in January 1984.[](#cite_note-69) There have been ongoing negotiations to open more CANDU 6 reactors in the country, including a 2007 deal between Canada, China and Argentina, but to date no firm plans have been announced.[](#cite_note-70)

A licensing agreement with Romania was signed in 1977, selling the CANDU 6 design for $5 million per reactor for the first four reactors, and then $2 million each for the next twelve. In addition, Canadian companies would supply a varying amount of equipment for the reactors, about $100 million of the first reactor's $800 million price tag, and then falling over time. In 1980, [Nicolae Ceaușescu](/wiki/Nicolae_Ceau%C8%99escu "Nicolae Ceaușescu") asked for a modification to provide goods instead of cash, in exchange the amount of Canadian content was increased and a second reactor would be built with Canadian help. Economic troubles in the country worsened throughout the construction phase. The first reactor of the [Cernavodă Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Cernavod%C4%83_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Cernavodă Nuclear Power Plant") only came online in April 1996, a decade after its December 1985 predicted startup.[](#cite_note-71) Further loans were arranged for completion of the second reactor, which went online in November 2007.[](#cite_note-72)

In January 1975, a deal was announced for a single CANDU 6 reactor to be built in South Korea, now known as the [Wolsong-1 Power Reactor](/wiki/Wolseong_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Wolseong Nuclear Power Plant"). Construction started in 1977 and commercial operation began in April 1983. In December 1990 a further deal was announced for three additional units at the same site, which began operation in the period 1997–1999.[](#cite_note-73) South Korea also negotiated development and technology transfer deals with Westinghouse for their advanced System-80 reactor design, and all future development is based on locally built versions of this reactor.[](#cite_note-74)

In June 1998, construction started on a CANDU 6 reactor in Qinshan China [Qinshan Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Qinshan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Qinshan Nuclear Power Plant"), as Phase III (units 4 and 5) of the planned 11 unit facility. Commercial operation began in December 2002 and July 2003, respectively. These are the first heavy water reactors in China. Qinshan was the first CANDU-6 project to use open-top reactor building construction, and the first project where commercial operation began earlier than the projected date.[](#cite_note-75)

### Economic performance

The cost of electricity from any power plant can be calculated by roughly the same selection of factors: capital costs for construction or the payments on loans made to secure that capital, the cost of fuel on a per-watt-hour basis, and fixed and variable maintenance fees. In the case of nuclear power, one normally includes two additional costs, the cost of permanent waste disposal, and the cost of decommissioning the plant when its useful lifetime is over. Generally, the capital costs dominate the price of nuclear power, as the amount of power produced is so large that it overwhelms the cost of fuel and maintenance.[](#cite_note-76) The [World Nuclear Association](/wiki/World_Nuclear_Association "World Nuclear Association") calculates that the cost of fuel, including all processing, accounts for less than one cent (US$0.01) per kWh.[](#cite_note-econ-77)

Information on economic performance on CANDU is somewhat lopsided; the majority of reactors are in Ontario, which is also the "most public" among the major CANDU operators. Several anti-nuclear organizations like the Ontario Clean Air Alliance (OCAA) and Pembina have claimed that every CANDU design in Ontario went over budget by at least 25%, and average over 150% higher than estimated.[](#cite_note-apexa-78) However, this is predicated on using "dollar of the day" figures that are not adjusted for inflation. With inflation accounted for, all plants were on or under budget with the exception of Darlington.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Even accounting for inflation, Darlington went far over budget, at almost double the original estimate, but this project was stopped in-progress thereby incurring additional interest charges during a period of high interest rates, which is a special situation that was not expected to repeat itself.[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

In the 1980s, the pressure tubes in the Pickering A reactors were replaced ahead of design life due to unexpected deterioration caused by [hydrogen embrittlement](/wiki/Hydrogen_embrittlement "Hydrogen embrittlement"). Extensive inspection and maintenance has avoided this problem in later reactors. 

All the Pickering A and Bruce A reactors were shut down in 1999 in order to focus on restoring operational performance in the later generations at Pickering, Bruce, and Darlington. Before restarting the Pickering A reactors, OPG undertook a limited refurbishment program. The original cost and time estimates based on inadequate project scope development were greatly below the actual time and cost and it was determined that Pickering units 2 and 3 would not be restarted for commercial reasons. 

These overruns were repeated at Bruce, with Units 3 and 4 running 90% over budget.[](#cite_note-apexa-78) Similar overruns were experienced at Point Lepreau,[](#cite_note-79) and Gentilly-2 plant was shut down on 28 December 2012.[](#cite_note-80)

Based on the projected capital costs, and the low cost of fuel and in-service maintenance, in 1994 power from CANDU was predicted to be well under 5 cents/kWh.[](#cite_note-81)

In 1999, Ontario Hydro was broken up and its generation facilities re-formed into [Ontario Power Generation](/wiki/Ontario_Power_Generation "Ontario Power Generation") (OPG). In order to make the successor companies more attractive for private investors, $19.4 billion in "stranded debt" was placed in the control of the Ontario Electricity Financial Corporation. This debt is slowly paid down through a variety of sources, including a 0.7-cent/kWh tariff on all power, all income taxes paid by all operating companies, and all dividends paid by the OPG and [Hydro One](/wiki/Hydro_One "Hydro One"). 

In February 2026, the major refurbishment project of all four units of the Darlington plant was completed, under budget and four months ahead of schedule[](#cite_note-82). 

Darlington Units 1, 3 and 4 have operated with an average lifetime annual capacity factor of 85% and Unit 2 with a capacity factor of 78%,[](#cite_note-83) As of 2010[[update]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=CANDU_reactor&action=edit), refurbished units at Pickering and Bruce had lifetime capacity factors between 59 and 69%.[](#cite_note-g5-84) This includes periods of several years while the units were shut down for the retubing and refurbishing. Post-refurbishment capacity factors are much higher with Bruce A1 at 90.78%, Bruce A2 at 90.38% (2013+),[](#cite_note-pris_iaea_org-17) Pickering A1 at 71.18% and Pickering A4 at 70.38%.[](#cite_note-85) In 2009, Bruce A units 3 and 4 had capacity factors of 80.5% and 76.7% respectively, in a year when they had a major Vacuum Building outage.[](#cite_note-86)

## CANDU power plants

Today there are 26 CANDU reactors in use around the world, and 18 "[CANDU-derivatives](/wiki/IPHWR "IPHWR")" in India, developed from the CANDU design. After India [detonated a nuclear bomb in 1974](/wiki/Smiling_Buddha "Smiling Buddha"), Canada stopped nuclear dealings with India and India developed 18 operational and 1 under construction "[CANDU-derivatives](/wiki/IPHWR "IPHWR")". ### Owners' group

Several Canadian operators of CANDU reactors formed the CANDU Reactors Owners' Group in 1984;[](#cite_note-94) they were later joined by international operators, and, in 2025, rebranded as Conexus Nuclear Inc.[](#cite_note-95)

The organization collaborates in several areas to promote the CANDU reactor technology: 

  1. research and development[](#cite_note-96) This included base funding for part of the research and development costs of AECL.[](#cite_note-97)
  2. joint projects and services[](#cite_note-What-98)
  3. nuclear safety and environmental affairs[](#cite_note-What-98)
  4. information exchange[](#cite_note-What-98)

## Upgrade & Refurbish

Because CANDU reactors use a unique "**Calandria and Pressure Tube** " design rather than a single massive pressure vessel, they possess a distinct advantage: their core components can be entirely stripped out and replaced. This mid-life overhaul, known in the industry as **Refurbishment or Retubing** , effectively resets the reactor's clock, extending its operational life for another **30 to 36 years** at a fraction of the cost of building a new plant.[](#cite_note-99) [](#cite_note-100) [](#cite_note-101)

### Canada: The Epicenter of the Upgrade Supercycle

Canada is currently executing one of the largest clean-energy infrastructure projects in the world across its massive Ontario fleet. 

  * **Bruce Power MCR** (Major Component Replacement): The [Bruce Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Bruce_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Bruce Nuclear Generating Station") (the second-largest nuclear plant in the world) is undergoing a $13 billion mega-project to upgrade six of its units. Units 6 and 3 have already completed their overhauls ahead of schedule in the mid-2020s, with Units 4, 5, 7, and 8 systematically lined up through the late 2020s and early 2030s.
  * **Darlington Nuclear Refurbishment** : Ontario Power Generation (OPG) is successfully wrapping up its four-unit refurbishment project at [Darlington](/wiki/Darlington_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Darlington Nuclear Generating Station"). The project has become a global gold standard for nuclear megaprojects, consistently delivering upgraded units ahead of schedule and under budget using advanced 3D digital twinning and heavily automated robotic tooling.[](#cite_note-102)
  * **Pickering Extension** : Driven by skyrocketing power demands, the Ontario government announced plans to refurbish the massive [Pickering Nuclear Generating Station](/wiki/Pickering_Nuclear_Generating_Station "Pickering Nuclear Generating Station") (Units 5–8), shifting it from planned decommissioning to a life-extension track that will secure power into the 2060s.

### Romania: Securing Eastern European Energy Sovereignty

Romania is heavily reliant on its [Cernavodă Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Cernavod%C4%83_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Cernavodă Nuclear Power Plant"), which utilizes two Canadian-designed CANDU-6 reactors to provide roughly 20% of the country’s electricity. 

  * **Cernavodă Unit 1 Refurbishment** : Nuclearelectrica (Romania's state nuclear utility) has partnered with Canada's [AtkinsRéalis](/wiki/AtkinsR%C3%A9alis "AtkinsRéalis") (formerly SNC-Lavalin, the commercial steward of CANDU tech) and U.S. firms like **Fluor** to initiate the full refurbishment of Unit 1.
  * The Strategic Goal: The $2 billion project will shut down the reactor for retubing in the late 2020s, upgrading its control systems and safety mechanisms to Gen-III+ standards and extending its operation to 2060. This is viewed by the West as a vital project to decouple Eastern Europe from regional fossil fuel volatility.

### South Korea: The Wolsong Fleet Modernization

South Korea operates three active CANDU-6 heavy-water reactors at the [Wolsong Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Wolsong_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Wolsong Nuclear Power Plant") (Wolsong Units 2, 3, and 4). 

  * **The Policy Pivot** : After years of anti-nuclear policies under a previous administration, Seoul completely reversed course in the mid-2020s, prioritizing nuclear life-extensions.
  * **Upgrades and Optimization** : [Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power](/wiki/Korea_Hydro_%26_Nuclear_Power "Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power") (KHNP) is actively pursuing life-extension assessments and engineering upgrades for the Wolsong fleet. Because South Korea has a highly sophisticated domestic nuclear supply chain (which successfully built CANDU components in the past), they are utilizing a mix of Canadian engineering design and localized manufacturing to upgrade the reactors' digital control systems and safety margins.

### China: The Qinshan Phase III Breakthrough

China’s two CANDU-6 units at [Qinshan Nuclear Power Plant](/wiki/Qinshan_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Qinshan Nuclear Power Plant") Phase III have entered their 20-year to 30-year operational window, making them prime targets for the global upgrade cycle.[](#cite_note-103)

  * The Partnership:In 2024, China signed major commercial agreements with Canada's AtkinsRéalis to deploy highly specialized, automated robotic tooling systems to extract and replace the 380 highly radioactive fuel channels in the Qinshan cores.[](#cite_note-104)
  * The Fuel Revolution: What makes the Chinese upgrade unique globally is the integration of **Advanced Fuel Technology**. China has modified its upgraded CANDU reactors to run on **Recycled Uranium (RU)** salvaged from the spent fuel of neighboring Light-Water pressurized reactors. This upgrade allows China to wring an immense amount of extra energy out of discarded nuclear waste, creating a highly efficient, closed-loop system.
