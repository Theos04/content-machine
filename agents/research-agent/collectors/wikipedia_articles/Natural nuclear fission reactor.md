A **natural nuclear fission reactor** is a [uranium](/wiki/Uranium "Uranium") [deposit](/wiki/Mineral_deposit "Mineral deposit") where self-sustaining [nuclear chain reactions](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction "Nuclear chain reaction") occur. The idea of a [nuclear reactor](/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor") existing _in situ_ within an ore body moderated by groundwater was briefly explored by [Paul Kuroda](/wiki/Paul_Kuroda "Paul Kuroda") in 1956.[](#cite_note-1) The existence of an extinct, or **fossil** , **nuclear fission reactor** , where self-sustaining nuclear reactions occurred in the past, was established by analysis of [isotope](/wiki/Isotope "Isotope") [ratios](/wiki/Ratio "Ratio") of uranium and of the [fission products](/wiki/Fission_product "Fission product") (and the stable [daughter nuclides](/wiki/Daughter_nuclide "Daughter nuclide") of those fission products). The first discovery of such a reactor happened in 1972 in [Oklo](/wiki/Oklo "Oklo"), [Gabon](/wiki/Gabon "Gabon"), by researchers from the [French Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/French_Atomic_Energy_Commission "French Atomic Energy Commission") (CEA) when chemists performing quality control for the French nuclear industry noticed sharp depletions of [fissile](/wiki/Fissile "Fissile") [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") in gaseous [uranium hexafluoride](/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride "Uranium hexafluoride") made from Gabonese ore. 

Oklo is the only location where this phenomenon is known to have occurred, consisting of sixteen sites with patches of centimeter-sized [ore layers](/wiki/Ore_deposit "Ore deposit"). There, self-sustaining [nuclear fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") reactions are thought to have taken place approximately 1.7 [billion](/wiki/Billion "Billion") years ago, during the [Statherian](/wiki/Statherian "Statherian") period of the [Paleoproterozoic](/wiki/Paleoproterozoic "Paleoproterozoic"). Fission in the ore at Oklo continued off and on for a few hundred thousand years and probably never exceeded 100 [kW](/wiki/Watt#Kilowatt "Watt") of thermal power.[](#cite_note-2)[](#cite_note-3)[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4) Life on Earth at this time consisted largely of sea-bound [algae](/wiki/Algae "Algae") and the first [eukaryotes](/wiki/Eukaryote "Eukaryote"), living under a 2% [oxygen](/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") atmosphere. However, even this meager oxygen was likely essential to the concentration of uranium into fissionable ore bodies, as uranium dissolves in water only in the presence of oxygen. Before the planetary-scale production of oxygen by the early [photosynthesizers](/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis"), groundwater-moderated natural nuclear reactors are not thought to have been possible.[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4)

## Discovery of the Oklo fossil reactors

In May 1972, at the [Tricastin uranium enrichment site](/wiki/Tricastin_Nuclear_Power_Plant "Tricastin Nuclear Power Plant") at Pierrelatte, France, routine [mass spectrometry](/wiki/Mass_spectrometry "Mass spectrometry") comparing [UF6](/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride "Uranium hexafluoride") samples from the Oklo mine showed a discrepancy in the amount of the [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") isotope. Where the usual concentrations of [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") were 0.72% the Oklo samples showed only 0.60%. This was a significant difference—the samples bore 17% less [235  
U](/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") than expected.[](#cite_note-DavisGould2014-5) This discrepancy required explanation, as all civilian uranium handling facilities must meticulously account for all fissionable isotopes to ensure that none are diverted into the construction of unsanctioned [nuclear weapons](/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon"). Further, as [fissile material](/wiki/Fissile_material "Fissile material") is the reason for mining uranium in the first place, the missing 17% was also of direct economic concern.

Thus, the [French Atomic Energy Commission](/wiki/French_Atomic_Energy_Commission "French Atomic Energy Commission") (CEA) began an investigation. A series of measurements of the relative abundances of the two most significant [isotopes of uranium](/wiki/Isotopes_of_uranium "Isotopes of uranium") mined at Oklo showed anomalous results compared to those obtained for uranium from other mines. Further investigations into this uranium deposit discovered uranium ore with a 235  
U concentration as low as 0.44% (almost 40% below the normal value). Subsequent examination of isotopes of fission products such as [neodymium](/wiki/Neodymium "Neodymium") and [ruthenium](/wiki/Ruthenium "Ruthenium") also showed anomalies, as described in more detail below. However, the [trace radioisotope](/wiki/Trace_radioisotope "Trace radioisotope") 234  
U did not deviate significantly in its concentration from other natural samples. Both [depleted uranium](/wiki/Depleted_uranium "Depleted uranium") and [reprocessed uranium](/wiki/Reprocessed_uranium "Reprocessed uranium") will usually have 234  
U concentrations significantly different from the [secular equilibrium](/wiki/Secular_equilibrium "Secular equilibrium") of 55 [ppm](/wiki/Parts_per_million "Parts per million") 234  
U relative to 238  
U. This is due to 234  
U being enriched together with 235  
U and due to it being both consumed by [neutron capture](/wiki/Neutron_capture "Neutron capture") and produced from 235  
U by [fast-neutron](/wiki/Fast_neutron "Fast neutron")-induced (n,2n) reactions in nuclear reactors. In Oklo, any possible deviation of 234  
U concentration present at the time the reactor was active would have long since decayed away. 236  
U must have also been present in higher-than-usual ratios during the time the reactor was operating, but due to its half-life of 2.348×107 years being almost two orders of magnitude shorter than the time elapsed since the reactor operated, it has decayed to roughly 1.4×10−22 its original value and below any abilities of current equipment to detect. 

This loss in 235  
U is exactly what happens in a nuclear reactor. A possible explanation was that the uranium ore had operated as a natural fission reactor in the distant geological past. Other observations led to the same conclusion, and on 25 September 1972, the CEA announced their finding that self-sustaining nuclear chain reactions had occurred on Earth about 2 billion years ago. Later, other natural nuclear fission reactors were discovered in the region.[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4)

## Fission product isotope signatures

[](/wiki/File:Neodymium_isotope_signatures.svg)Isotope signatures of natural neodymium and fission product neodymium from 235  
U which had been subjected to thermal neutrons.

### Neodymium

The neodymium (Nd) found at Oklo has a different isotopic composition to that of natural neodymium: the latter contains 27% 142  
Nd, while that of Oklo contains less than 6%. The 142  
Nd is not produced by fission; the ore contains both fission-produced and natural neodymium. From this 142  
Nd content, we can subtract the natural neodymium and gain access to the isotopic composition of neodymium produced by the fission of 235  
U. The two isotopes 143  
Nd and 145  
Nd lead to the formation of 144  
Nd and 146  
Nd by neutron capture. This excess must be corrected (see above) to obtain agreement between this corrected isotopic composition and that deduced from fission yields. 

### Ruthenium

[](/wiki/File:Ruthenium_isotope_signatures.svg)Isotope signatures of natural ruthenium and fission product ruthenium from 235  
U which had been subjected to thermal neutrons. The 100  
Mo (an extremely long-lived [double beta emitter](/wiki/Double_beta_decay "Double beta decay")) has not had time to decay to 100  
Ru in more than trace quantities over the time since the reactors stopped working.

Similar investigations into the isotopic ratios of [ruthenium](/wiki/Ruthenium "Ruthenium") (Ru) at Oklo found a much higher 99  
Ru concentration than otherwise naturally occurring (27–30% vs. 12.7%). This anomaly could be explained by the decay of [99  
Tc](/wiki/Technetium-99 "Technetium-99") to 99  
Ru. In the bar chart, the normal natural isotope signature of ruthenium is compared with that for [fission product](/wiki/Fission_product "Fission product") ruthenium which is the result of the [fission](/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") of 235  
U with thermal neutrons. The fission ruthenium has a different isotope signature. The level of 100  
Ru in the fission product mixture is low because fission produces [neutron rich isotopes](/wiki/Neutron_excess "Neutron excess") which subsequently [beta decay](/wiki/Beta_decay "Beta decay") and 100  
Ru would only be produced in appreciable quantities by [double beta decay](/wiki/Double_beta_decay "Double beta decay") of the very long-lived (half-life 7.1×1018 years) [molybdenum](/wiki/Molybdenum "Molybdenum") isotope 100  
Mo. On the timescale of when the reactors were in operation, very little (about 0.17 [ppb](/wiki/Parts_per_billion "Parts per billion")) decay to 100  
Ru will have occurred. Other pathways of 100  
Ru production like [neutron capture](/wiki/Neutron_capture "Neutron capture") in 99  
Ru or 99  
Tc (quickly followed by beta decay) can only have occurred during high [neutron flux](/wiki/Neutron_flux "Neutron flux") and thus ceased when the fission chain reaction stopped. 

## Mechanism

The natural nuclear reactor at Oklo formed when a uranium-rich mineral deposit became inundated with [groundwater](/wiki/Groundwater "Groundwater"), which could act as a [moderator](/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") for the neutrons produced by nuclear fission. A [chain reaction](/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction "Nuclear chain reaction") took place, producing heat that caused the groundwater to boil away; without a moderator that could slow the neutrons, however, the reaction slowed or stopped. The reactor thus had a negative [void coefficient](/wiki/Void_coefficient "Void coefficient") of reactivity, something employed as a safety mechanism in human-made [light water reactors](/wiki/Light_water_reactor "Light water reactor"). After cooling of the mineral deposit, the water returned, and the reaction restarted, completing a full cycle every 3 hours. The fission reaction cycles continued for hundreds of thousands of years and ended when the ever-decreasing fissile materials, coupled with the build-up of [neutron poisons](/wiki/Neutron_poison "Neutron poison"), no longer could sustain a chain reaction. 

Fission of uranium normally produces five known isotopes of the fission-product gas [xenon](/wiki/Xenon "Xenon"); all five have been found trapped in the remnants of the natural reactor, in varying concentrations. The concentrations of xenon isotopes, found trapped in mineral formations 2 billion years later, make it possible to calculate the specific time intervals of reactor operation: approximately 30 minutes of criticality followed by 2 hours and 30 minutes of cooling down (exponentially decreasing residual [decay heat](/wiki/Decay_heat "Decay heat")) to complete a 3-hour cycle.[](#cite_note-6) [Xenon-135](/wiki/Xenon-135 "Xenon-135") is the strongest known neutron poison. However, it is not produced directly in appreciable amounts but rather as a decay product of [iodine-135](/wiki/Iodine-135 "Iodine-135") (or one of its [parent nuclides](/wiki/Parent_nuclide "Parent nuclide")). Xenon-135 itself is unstable and decays to [caesium-135](/wiki/Caesium-135 "Caesium-135") if not allowed to absorb neutrons. While caesium-135 is relatively long lived, all caesium-135 produced by the Oklo reactor has since decayed further to stable [barium-135](/wiki/Barium-135 "Barium-135"). Meanwhile, xenon-136, the product of [neutron capture](/wiki/Neutron_capture "Neutron capture") in xenon-135 decays extremely slowly via [double beta decay](/wiki/Double_beta_decay "Double beta decay") and thus scientists were able to determine the neutronics of this reactor by calculations based on those isotope ratios almost two billion years after it stopped fissioning uranium. 

[](/wiki/File:Anteil_Uran235_im_Natururan.png)Change of content of Uranium-235 in natural uranium; the content was 3.65% 2 billion years ago.

A key factor that made the reaction possible was that, at the time the reactor went [critical](/wiki/Critical_mass "Critical mass") 1.7 billion years ago, the [fissile](/wiki/Fissile "Fissile") isotope 235  
U made up about 3.1% of the natural uranium, which is comparable to the amount used in some of today's reactors. (The remaining 96.9% was 238  
U and roughly 55 ppm 234  
U, neither of which is fissile by [slow](/wiki/Neutron_temperature "Neutron temperature") or moderated neutrons.) Because 235  
U has a shorter [half-life](/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") than 238  
U, and thus decays more rapidly, the current abundance of 235  
U in natural uranium is only 0.72%. A natural nuclear reactor is therefore no longer possible on Earth without [heavy water](/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water") or [graphite](/wiki/Graphite "Graphite").[](#cite_note-Greenwood1257-7)

The Oklo uranium ore deposits are the only known sites in which natural nuclear reactors existed. Other rich uranium ore bodies would also have had sufficient uranium to support nuclear reactions at that time, but the combination of uranium, water, and physical conditions needed to support the chain reaction was unique, as far as is currently known, to the Oklo ore bodies. It is also possible that other natural nuclear fission reactors were once operating but have since been geologically disturbed so much as to be unrecognizable, possibly even "diluting" the uranium so far that the isotope ratio would no longer serve as a "fingerprint". Only a small part of the continental crust and no part of the oceanic crust reaches the age of the deposits at Oklo or an age during which isotope ratios of natural uranium would have allowed a self-sustaining chain reaction with water as a moderator. 

Another factor which probably contributed to the start of the Oklo natural nuclear reactor at 2 billion years, rather than earlier, was the [increasing oxygen content in the Earth's atmosphere](/wiki/Great_Oxygenation_Event "Great Oxygenation Event").[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4) Uranium is naturally present in the rocks of the earth, and the abundance of fissile 235  
U was at least 3% or higher at all times prior to reactor startup. Uranium is soluble in water only in the presence of [oxygen](/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen").[_(/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Therefore, increasing oxygen levels during the aging of the Earth may have allowed uranium to be dissolved and transported with groundwater to places where a high enough concentration could accumulate to form rich uranium ore bodies. Without the new aerobic environment available on Earth at the time, these concentrations probably could not have taken place. 

It is estimated that nuclear reactions in the uranium in centimeter- to meter-sized veins consumed about five tons of 235  
U and elevated temperatures to a few hundred degrees Celsius.[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4)[](#cite_note-8) Most of the non-volatile fission products and actinides have only moved centimeters in the veins during the last 2 billion years.[](#cite_note-Gauthier-Lafaye1996-4) Studies have suggested this as a useful natural analogue for nuclear waste disposal.[](#cite_note-9) The overall [mass defect](/wiki/Mass_defect "Mass defect") from the fission of five tons of 235  
U is about 4.6 kilograms (10 lb). Over its lifetime the reactor produced roughly 100 megatonnes of TNT (420 PJ) in thermal energy, including [neutrinos](/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino"). If one ignores fission of plutonium (which makes up roughly a third of fission events over the course of normal burnup in modern human-made [light water reactors](/wiki/Light_water_reactor "Light water reactor")), then [fission product yields](/wiki/Fission_product_yield "Fission product yield") amount to roughly 129 kilograms (284 lb) of technetium-99 (since decayed to ruthenium-99), 108 kilograms (238 lb) of [zirconium-93](/wiki/Zirconium-93 "Zirconium-93") (since decayed to [niobium](/wiki/Niobium "Niobium")-93), 198 kilograms (437 lb) of caesium-135 (since decayed to barium-135, but the real value is probably lower as its parent nuclide, xenon-135, is a strong neutron poison and will have absorbed neutrons before decaying to 135  
Cs in some cases), 28 kilograms (62 lb) of [palladium-107](/wiki/Palladium-107 "Palladium-107") (since decayed to silver), 86 kilograms (190 lb) of [strontium-90](/wiki/Strontium-90 "Strontium-90") (long since decayed to zirconium), and 185 kilograms (408 lb) of [caesium-137](/wiki/Caesium-137 "Caesium-137") (long since decayed to barium). 

## Relation to the atomic fine-structure constant

The natural reactor of Oklo has been used to check if the atomic [fine-structure constant](/wiki/Fine-structure_constant "Fine-structure constant") _α_ might have changed over the past 2 billion years. That is because α influences the rate of various nuclear reactions. For example, [149  
Sm](/wiki/Samarium-149 "Samarium-149") captures a neutron to become 150  
Sm, and since the rate of neutron capture depends on the value of _α_ , the ratio of the two [samarium](/wiki/Samarium "Samarium") isotopes in samples from Oklo can be used to calculate the value of _α_ from 2 billion years ago. 

Several studies have analysed the relative concentrations of radioactive isotopes left behind at Oklo, and most have concluded that nuclear reactions then were much the same as they are today, which implies that _α_ was the same too.[](#cite_note-10)[](#cite_note-11)[](#cite_note-12)
